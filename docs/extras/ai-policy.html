Using AI tools on this project

One principle, a table, and the one problem this project has that almost no other student project has.

Every research project now needs a page like this. Yours needs it more than most, because the thing you are studying is a language model and the tool sitting open in the other tab is also a language model. That is not an awkward coincidence to be handled in a footnote. It is the most interesting methodological problem in your study, and getting it right is a large part of what will make anyone believe your numbers.

By the end of this page you can

  • State the principle in one sentence and apply it to a case you have not seen before.
  • Say which uses are fine, which are not, and give the reason for each rather than the rule.
  • Explain the circularity problem out loud: why a model-written dataset or a model-assigned label changes the question the study answers.
  • Name what the recall denominator actually counts, and whose opinion it encodes.
  • Write a disclosure line in a progress log and an AI-use statement in the write-up.
  • Argue both sides of the genuinely hard case — a model helping write the experimental prompts — and say what makes it defensible.
  • Find and follow the rules that override this page before you submit anything anywhere.

1 · The principle

Stated once, and everything else follows from it

AI tools are allowed, and for some things actively encouraged, when they help you learn or do mechanical work. They are not allowed to produce the things that constitute the research contribution: the dataset, the labels, the interpretation, the limitations.

The test is not did I use AI. The test is: can I defend every claim in this write-up as my own understanding, out loud, under questioning, with the document closed?

That test is worth taking seriously as a description of what you are building this year, not as a rule you are being made to obey. The repository will bit-rot. The charts will be regenerated. What survives is a person who can stand up, say what the confusion matrix showed, say why recall was the headline metric and not accuracy, and answer a hostile follow-up about whether the effect could have come from her own writing habits. A judge at a science fair is trying, with about six minutes of questions, to find out whether that person exists. So is a reviewer. So is your mentor at every check-in.

Which is exactly why these tools are useful here rather than merely tolerated. A patient explainer that will rephrase the same idea six different ways is a real asset to someone learning statistics alone in a bedroom. The failure mode is not use. The failure mode is finishing the year with an artifact and no capacity.

2 · Allowed, not allowed, and why

The third column is the one that matters. If you only remember the middle column you will have to come back and look things up; if you understand the third column you can decide new cases yourself, which is the point.

The useAllowed?Why
Asking for a concept you did not understand, in different words. "Explain what a false negative is as if I had never seen a table." Yes The understanding ends up on your side of the table. That understanding is the thing being built; nothing is substituted for it.
Pasting an error message or traceback and asking what it means. Yes A dictionary lookup with better manners. Nothing about your research is at stake in the difference between a KeyError and a FileNotFoundError.
Generating boilerplate: reading a CSV, argument parsing, axis labels and figure sizes in analyze.py. Yes, with review Plumbing is not a finding. But you must be able to explain every line you keep, and the tests must pass — see the box below. A chart you cannot account for axis by axis is one question away from unravelling the whole defence.
Drafting an email, a schedule, an abstract-length summary of a paper you already read. Yes No research content is being produced. Nobody is claiming your emails are original work.
Having a model quiz you, or arguing with it about whether your design is sound. Yes This is studying. Apply the test the next day, cold: if you cannot reproduce the argument on a blank page, the session was substitution wearing study's clothes.
Writing any of the forty student responses. No They are the dataset. A model-written dataset makes the study circular: you would be testing whether one model can detect the misconceptions another model wrote in. That is a different question, and a much less interesting one. Section 3.
Topping up: you wrote thirty-four and want a model to produce the last six. No Worse than the whole-dataset case, not better. Six items from a different generative process sit inside your positive class as a confound you can never separate out again.
Assigning the M / C labels. No The labels are the ground truth. If a model produces them you are measuring model-model agreement, not accuracy. Everything downstream inherits that.
Using a model as the blind second reader for the agreement check in Phase 3. No The second reader exists to test whether a human who was not you can recover your labels from the text alone. A model agreeing with you does not establish that.
Writing the interpretation of the results. No That paragraph is the contribution. Everything before it is arithmetic that a script already did. Saying what the numbers mean, and what they do not mean, is the research.
Writing the Limitations section. No This is where you demonstrate that you understand your own study. A model can produce the generic three — small sample, one model, one domain — and will miss every limitation that is specific to what you actually did. Exercise 5.
Polishing prose you wrote: tightening a clumsy sentence, fixing a comma splice. Yes, with care Editing is not authorship. The order is absolute: content first, always. If the polished version says something you had not decided to say, you have crossed over and should put the sentence back.
Suggesting wordings for the two experimental prompts. Genuinely hard The prompt is the independent variable, so this is closer to helping design the experiment than to helping write boilerplate. Section 5 argues it through.
If a tool touched your code, run these before you commit 
$ cd python
$ python3 misconception/run_experiment.py --mock --sample
$ python3 misconception/analyze.py
$ pytest -q          # from the repository root

The suite cross-checks every metric against scikit-learn, so a plausible-looking rewrite of compute_metrics that swaps precision and recall fails here rather than in April. Remember that anything produced by --mock or --sample is demo data: useful for proving the plumbing works, never reportable as a result.

3 · The circularity problem

This is the sharpest idea on the page. Everything in section 2 about the dataset and the labels is one argument, applied twice.

Your study compares two prompts on forty responses. The comparison has meaning only because of what sits at each end of it. At one end are forty pieces of writing that resemble what a confused sixteen-year-old would actually say about passwords. At the other end are forty labels saying which of those contain a misconception, assigned by someone who understands the misconception. The model under test is measured against both. If either end is produced by a language model, the measurement still produces a number, and the number no longer answers the question you asked.

Take the labels first, because the arithmetic makes it obvious

Recall, with M — "this response contains a misconception" — as the positive class, is

$$\text{recall} \;=\; \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$$

Read the denominator carefully. TP + FN is not "the responses that contain a misconception". It is the responses that the labeller said contain a misconception. Every metric in Phase 5 is arithmetic on a table whose rows are somebody's judgement. When that somebody is you, having read each response cold and written down a decision you can justify, recall means what you want it to mean: of the misconceptions that were really there, what fraction did the model catch.

When that somebody is a model, recall becomes: of the items the labelling model called M, what fraction did the tested model also call M. That is an agreement statistic between two models. It will probably look good, because two language models trained on overlapping text tend to be confused by the same things and confident about the same things — which means their errors are correlated, which means the agreement is high for a reason that has nothing to do with correctness. You would report 90% recall and have measured a family resemblance.

Now the dataset, which is subtler and worse

Suppose you ask a model: write eight responses in which a student wrongly believes MFA makes an account impossible to hack. You get eight fluent, plausible paragraphs. Here is what has quietly happened.

Those paragraphs contain the features that that model associates with that misconception — its own paraphrase of the idea, in its own register, with its own tells. Phase 3 already warned you about tells: the M items shout always, never, 100% safe, and the C items murmur generally, it depends. A model asked to write wrong answers produces that pattern more reliably than you would, not less, because it is producing a recognisable genre rather than a recollection of a real student.

Then you run the misconception-aware condition, which hands the model a catalogue entry phrased in the same vocabulary the items were generated from. Recall goes to the mid-nineties in both conditions. There is no room above that for the catalogue to show an effect, so your headline comparison is dead — and if it does show an effect, you cannot tell whether the catalogue supplied knowledge or supplied a keyword match. You have measured whether a model can recognise its own writing, which was never in doubt.

Circular, precisely

A study is circular when something being measured also determines what counts as the right answer. The measurement then cannot come out badly, so it carries no information — a test that everything passes tells you nothing about anything.

This is not a rule invented for school projects. Model-generated benchmarks and model-as-judge evaluation are a live, unresolved argument in machine-learning research right now cited. If you want to say anything about that argument in your write-up, find and read an actual paper on it and cite that paper — do not cite this page, which is a summary written for you and not evidence of anything.

Lab · the circularity visualiser

Choose who wrote the forty responses and who assigned the labels. The diagram is the inference chain your study rests on; a dashed red loop marks a path where the thing being tested also determines the right answer. The readout prints the question each configuration actually answers — not the question you intended to ask. Exactly one of the six configurations answers the intended one.

Notice what the visualiser does not say. It never says the circular configurations produce no number, or a low number. They produce a perfectly ordinary confusion matrix with perfectly ordinary metrics, and if you did not draw the chain you would have no way of telling from the output that anything was wrong. That is why this rule has to be applied before the run, not diagnosed after it.

4 · Disclosure

Two places, and both are short.

In every progress log, the Tools used line that is already at the bottom of progress/TEMPLATE.md. One plain sentence per use: what you asked, and what you did with the answer. Write none if none.

Template · progress log 
Tools used: asked an AI assistant <what you asked>, after <what
prompted the question>. Used the answer to <what you then did
yourself>. <Anything you checked it against.>

Good. "Asked an AI assistant why my recall came out as undefined rather than 0 when the model predicted C for everything. Understood that the denominator TP + FN was zero, checked it against the table by hand, and added the zero-denominator case to my Phase 5 notes so I do not report it as 0%."

Bad. "Used AI a bit this week."

The second is not bad because it admits less. It is bad because it is unusable: six weeks later neither of us can tell whether the undefined-metric distinction is something you know or something you were once told. The first takes twenty seconds and is a genuine part of the record.

In the final write-up, a short statement, normally at the end of the methods or just before the references. It is a paragraph, not a confession.

Template · write-up 
AI use statement. All forty student responses and all forty
M/C labels in this study were written by the author. No language
model contributed to the dataset, the labels, the interpretation
of the results, or the limitations. An AI assistant was used for
<list: e.g. explaining statistical concepts during study,
diagnosing Python errors, drafting plotting boilerplate that the
author reviewed, and copy-editing prose the author had already
written>. <If relevant: candidate wordings for the experimental
prompts were discussed with an AI assistant; every wording tried,
including those suggested by the assistant, is recorded in
progress/prompt-log.md.> The author is responsible for every
claim made here.

Disclosure is normal professional practice. Most journals and many competitions now require a statement of this kind, and their reason for requiring it is not suspicion — it is that a reader cannot evaluate a method they cannot see. The asymmetry is worth stating plainly: disclosure is never penalised; non-disclosure is the only thing that is. No quantity of disclosed use produces a worse outcome here, because disclosed use is a fact we can both work with. If the Tools used lines start showing that whole paragraphs are arriving from outside, the response is a conversation about how you are working, not a sanction. Undisclosed use is different in kind: it corrupts the record, and a research record that cannot be trusted is worth nothing at all.

Competition and school rules override this page

Science-fair programmes, your school's academic-integrity policy, and any journal or showcase you submit to each set their own rules about AI use and disclosure. Those rules are the binding authority. This page is not.

They also change, sometimes between one year and the next, which is exactly why no specific competition's rules are quoted here — a rule copied into a web page in one August is a liability the following spring. Before you submit anything anywhere: find the current rules for that specific venue, read the section on AI use and on disclosure forms, and bring anything ambiguous to your mentor rather than deciding alone. Doing this a week before a deadline is a normal part of submitting; doing it the night before is how projects get withdrawn.

5 · The genuinely hard one: prompts written with a model

Everything above was reasonably clear-cut. This one is not, and pretending otherwise would make the page less useful.

The two prompts in python/misconception/prompts.py are the independent variable. They are the single thing that differs between your two conditions. Everything else — model, temperature, responses, labels, parsing — is held fixed precisely so that any difference in the results can be attributed to them. So asking a model to help write them is not like asking for a CSV loader. It is closer to asking for help designing the experiment.

The case for forbidding it. If the treatment itself was authored elsewhere, what exactly did you contribute? And a model asked to "write a good prompt for detecting misconceptions" will produce something fluent that you did not reason your way to, which is awkward when a judge asks why the output-format instruction is repeated twice at the end.

The case against forbidding it. A prompt is not a claim. It is a piece of apparatus, and you were always going to arrive at it by trial: write one, run it on the twelve demo items, notice that a third of the replies are unparseable, make the format instruction blunter, run it again. That loop is legitimate whether the suggestion for the next wording comes from a model, from a friend, or from staring at the wall. Forbidding it outright would not stop it happening; it would only stop it being written down, which is strictly worse for everyone.

And here is where the real danger actually lives, which is not authorship at all. Suppose you try eleven wordings. Suppose you evaluate each one against your real forty responses, see which gives the misconception-aware condition its biggest lead, and report that one. Every step felt reasonable. The result is worthless, because you have selected the wording that flattered your hypothesis out of eleven chances to find one, and you would have stopped searching the moment a wording worked. That is researcher degrees of freedom — the same problem Phase 5 raises about choosing which metric to headline after seeing the numbers. The remedy is identical in both places, and it is not restraint. It is disclosure of the whole search.

The honest resolution
  • Allowed, including asking a model for candidate wordings.
  • Every prompt you try is recorded in progress/prompt-log.md: the wording, where it came from — your head, a model, an example you read — and why you kept or dropped it. Committing each version of prompts.py gives you the wordings for free in the git history; the log is for the reasons, which git cannot record.
  • Develop them on demo data — --mock --sample, the twelve items — not on your forty. Tuning a prompt against the real dataset is how a selection effect gets in.
  • Edit both prompts symmetrically. If the baseline gets a blunter format instruction, so does the misconception-aware one. The two must differ in exactly one respect: the catalogue.
  • Freeze before the real run. After you have seen the real results, a changed prompt means a new run that is exploratory and reported as such — not a replacement for the first one.

A reader who can see all eleven wordings and your reasons can judge your method. A reader shown only the winner cannot. The prompt log is what converts an uncomfortable grey area into an inspectable one, and it takes about ninety seconds a week to maintain.

6 · Exercises

All five are judgement calls, and none has a one-word answer. Argue from the third column of the table, not from the second.

Exercise 6.1 ★★thinking

You have written eighteen of the twenty M items and have run out of ideas. You ask a model: what do students typically get wrong about password managers? You take two of the ideas it lists, and write the two responses yourself, in your own words. Acceptable?

Solution

Acceptable, with disclosure and one real caveat. An idea is not the dataset. The forty pieces of writing are the dataset, and you wrote all forty. Nothing in the inference chain in section 3 has changed: the items are human-written, the labels are yours, and the model under test is measured against both.

The caveat is about the catalogue, which is a separate research artifact from the responses. The ten misconceptions are supposed to be things students actually believe, and the evidence for that should come from teachers, from published misconception research, or from your own observation. A misconception that exists only because a language model listed it is a conjecture about students, not an observation of them, and it should be marked as such. If you add one, note where it came from, and be able to say at the check-in which entries in misconceptions.csv have a source behind them and which do not.

Disclosure line: "Asked an AI assistant for common student misunderstandings about password managers when I ran out of ideas at eighteen items; used two of the ideas, wrote both responses myself, and flagged both catalogue entries as unsourced."

Exercise 6.2 ★★thinking

Phase 3 asks for a blind second reader so you can compute percentage agreement and Cohen's kappa. Nobody in your house wants to label forty responses about passwords. You ask a large model to do it instead, and report the resulting kappa. Acceptable?

Solution

Not acceptable, and the reason is worth being precise about, because the tempting objection — "but the model is not the model under test, so there is no circularity" — is half right.

It is right that this is not the labels problem from section 3: your labels are still yours, and the metrics still measure the tested model against your judgement. What breaks is what kappa means. The second reader exists to answer one question: is the distinction I claim to be labelling recoverable from the text by someone other than me? A high kappa with a second human says the construct is real and legible, not private to your head. A high kappa with a model says two systems agree, one of which has exactly the correlated-error problem section 3 describes; a low kappa tells you almost nothing, since you cannot ask the model what it was thinking and get a trustworthy answer.

The honest paths are: find one human — a classmate, a sibling, a teacher, twenty minutes each — or report that you could not obtain a second reader and name it in Limitations as a genuine weakness. The second option is not a failure. "The dataset was written and labelled by a single person, so no inter-rater agreement could be computed" is a sentence a judge respects, and it is exactly the kind of limitation Exercise 6.5 is about.

Exercise 6.3 ★★★thinking

You wrote all forty items and all forty labels yourself. Worried about the tells Phase 3 warned about, you paste the forty into a model with the labels removed and ask: which of these do you think are meant to contain an error, and what surface features gave it away? Acceptable? And if so, what do you do with the answer?

Solution

Acceptable, and it is a good idea — but the second half of the question is where the real content is, because there is a way to do this that quietly ruins the study.

Why it is fine in principle: you are running a diagnostic on your dataset, not producing it. The items and the labels remain yours. Nor is there any contamination worry of the kind people imagine: a call to Ollama is stateless, so a model that reads your items today remembers nothing about them during the real run.

The trap is different and sharper. Suppose the model correctly picks nineteen of your twenty M items and tells you it did so from the words always, never and completely. If you now go through and edit until that model can no longer tell, you have tuned the dataset against the thing you are measuring. Your forty items are no longer forty independent samples of student-like writing; they are forty items selected for being hard for one particular model, and the recall you report afterwards is a number about that tuning process, not about misconception detection.

The defensible version: treat the list of surface cues as a general style checklist. Apply it uniformly to all forty items — if always is banned it is banned in the C items too — do one pass, and stop. Do not iterate against the model's verdicts. Record what you did and the fact that you did one pass, because a reader who knows you screened for lexical tells trusts the dataset more, and a reader who discovers it later trusts it less.

Exercise 6.4 ★★arithmetic

Your real run finishes. Baseline recall is 11 of 20; misconception-aware recall is 13 of 20. You paste both confusion matrices into a model and ask what they mean. It replies that the misconception-aware prompt improved recall by ten percentage points, consistent with your hypothesis. Which part of that exchange was acceptable, and what is wrong with the answer?

Solution

Asking was fine; using the reply as your interpretation would not be. The line runs between "what does this number mean" — a definition question, allowed — and "tell me what I found", which is the contribution.

Now the substance, which is why you have to be the one writing this paragraph. The reply is arithmetically right and inferentially wrong. Recall is computed over the twenty M items only, so the sample size behind each figure is 20, not 40. Wilson intervals:

13/20 = 65%, 95% interval 43% to 82%.
11/20 = 55%, 95% interval 34% to 74%.

The intervals overlap across almost their entire length. The whole result rests on two items changing their verdict. A single response you might have labelled either way, or the ordinary run-to-run variation you will see when you run twice at temperature 0, is enough to erase it or double it.

So the honest interpretation is: recall was higher in the misconception-aware condition (13/20 versus 11/20), but with n = 20 in the positive class the confidence intervals overlap substantially and this pilot cannot distinguish a real effect from noise. The direction is consistent with the prediction registered in Phase 1; the magnitude is not established. A model will not volunteer that paragraph unless you already know to ask for it, which is the argument for writing it yourself.

Exercise 6.5 ★★★writing

You ask a model to draft the Limitations section, read what it produces, agree with most of it, and rewrite it entirely in your own words, keeping the points you agree with and deleting the rest. Nothing generated survives verbatim. Acceptable?

Solution

No — and this is the subtlest case on the page, because the usual objection does not apply. Nobody is claiming a generated sentence as their own; every word is yours.

The problem is that in a Limitations section the list is the work, not the prose. A model that has never seen your repository can produce the generic three: small sample, a single model, a single domain. Those are true, and a reader learns nothing from them, because they are true of every pilot ever run. What a judge is actually testing is whether you can name the limitations that are specific to this study — and those are only visible from inside it:

  • One person wrote both the responses and the labels, so the labels may encode that person's writing habits rather than an independent judgement.
  • The catalogue and the M items share vocabulary, so the misconception-aware prompt may be matching words rather than recognising concepts — the very thing the study claims to measure.
  • Two runs at temperature 0 differed on some items, so a difference of one or two verdicts is inside the noise floor of the instrument.
  • Unparseable replies were counted separately rather than defaulted to C, which keeps the metrics honest but means the denominators need stating every time.
  • The forty responses are what one person imagines a confused student writing, not collected student work.

Not one of those is available to a model, and every one of them is worth more than the generic three. If you start from a generated draft you will anchor on its list and stop thinking — the failure is not plagiarism, it is that you will not notice what is missing. Write it cold, from your own log, then use the tool afterwards to tighten sentences if you like. Content first, always.

7 · Verification

Before the check-in
  • I can state the principle in one sentence, without looking at this page.
  • I can explain, out loud, why a model-written dataset changes the question the study answers — and why the resulting numbers still look completely normal.
  • I can say what TP + FN counts and whose judgement it encodes.
  • Every progress log so far has a Tools used line, or the word none.
  • Every prompt wording I have tried is in progress/prompt-log.md with its origin and the reason I kept or dropped it.
  • I have found the current AI-use and disclosure rules for whatever I intend to submit to, and asked about anything ambiguous.
  • Everything in the write-up is something I could defend under questioning with the document closed.
Going deeper (optional)

The same scepticism points back at this site. These pages were written by a person and contain errors, and the errors that survive proofreading are the plausible ones. The epistemic chips are there partly for that reason: cited means somebody is being trusted, observed means a number came out of code you can rerun, proved means the arithmetic is on the page and you can check it. If a claim here disagrees with your own output, one of us is wrong, and finding out which is a good afternoon either way. Bring it to the call.

That disposition — provisional trust in every source, including the confident ones, including this one — is what the Tools used line is really training.
