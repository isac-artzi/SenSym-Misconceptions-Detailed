1 · The study

misconception

A wrong belief that is stable, confident and shared by many people — not a slip of the pen but a rule someone has built and applies consistently. "Changing my password every month keeps me safe" is a misconception; typing your password into the username box is a mistake. The difference matters because a misconception is predictable, and anything predictable can be catalogued and looked for.

In this project: ten of them live in python/data/misconceptions.csv. You meet them in Phase 3, where you write two M and two C responses for each.

misconception-aware prompting

Handing the model the catalogue of known misconceptions inside the prompt, before asking it to judge a response. It is the whole intervention this study tests, and it is named misconception_aware in CONDITIONS. The idea being tested is that naming a failure mode helps a model notice that failure mode — which would show up as recall rising more than precision.

You meet this in Phase 1, where the two prompts are set side by side and every difference between them is listed.

baseline condition

The arm you compare against: same model, same temperature, same forty responses, same output format, but a prompt that never mentions any specific misconception. It stands in for an ordinary chatbot. Without it, "recall was 70%" is a number with nothing to be measured against, and therefore not a result.

Named baseline in config.py. A test called test_baseline_prompt_does_not_leak_the_misconception_list in python/tests/test_pipeline.py checks that the plain condition really is plain.

condition

One of the versions being compared in an experiment. This study has exactly two: baseline and misconception-aware. The rule that makes a comparison mean anything is that exactly one thing differs between conditions; when two things differ, the result is confounded, which is not a weak result but no result at all.

positive class

The category the detector is trying to find, and the one that precision, recall and F1 are computed with respect to. Here it is M, "this response contains a misconception", set as POSITIVE_LABEL = LABEL_MISCONCEPTION. "Positive" does not mean good — a smoke alarm's positive class is smoke. A metric quoted without its positive class stated is meaningless, so state it every single time.

Flip the choice to C and every one of those three numbers changes; see specificity for what recall becomes when you do.

ground truth

The labels you have decided are correct, against which the model's answers are scored. It is not truth handed down by the universe: here it is your own reading of forty responses you wrote, which is exactly why it has to be checked by somebody else. Ground truth that no second person can reproduce is an opinion with a confident name.

You build it in Phase 3 and defend it with blind second labelling and inter-rater agreement.

label

The single letter attached to a response: M for contains a misconception, C for correct. Set once in config.py as LABEL_MISCONCEPTION = "M" and LABEL_CORRECT = "C" so the strings cannot drift apart across files. Your label is ground truth; the model's letter is a prediction. Keeping those two words apart saves a great deal of confusion in Phase 5.

pilot study

A small study run to find out whether a method works and what a full version would need, rather than to settle the question. At n = 40 this one can detect a large effect and cannot detect a small one. Calling it a pilot in the write-up is accurate rather than modest, and it is what licenses the honest framing: here is a method, here is what it showed at this scale, here is what a larger version would need.

ceiling effect

When a task is so easy that every condition scores near the maximum, so the difference you are looking for has no room to appear. If the baseline prompt already catches all twenty misconceptions, the aware prompt cannot beat it, and you have measured nothing about prompting — only that your items were obvious.

You meet this in Phase 3, and it is the reason your M responses have to be plausibly wrong rather than cartoonishly wrong. It is the single most common way a small study destroys itself before it runs.

researcher degrees of freedom

All the small choices left open after the data arrives — which metric to lead with, which items to drop as ambiguous, whether to count an unparseable reply as wrong — each of which can be made in the direction that helps. Nobody has to be dishonest for this to produce a result that is not there; making twenty reasonable-looking choices is enough.

The fix is to spend the freedom in advance: see pre-registration. This is why the Phase 1 prediction is written and committed before Phase 4 runs.

pre-registration

Writing down the hypothesis, the primary metric and the analysis plan before collecting the data, somewhere dated that you cannot quietly edit later. It does not stop you changing your mind; it stops you changing your mind invisibly. Anything you decide afterwards is still reportable, labelled as exploratory.

Yours is the prediction in Phase 1 — recall rises more than precision — committed to the repository before the real run. The commit hash and its date are the evidence.

provenance

The record of where a number came from: which model, which prompt, which data file, which date, which commit. A number without provenance cannot be defended, because you cannot answer the first question anyone asks about it. Numbers produced by --mock or --sample are demo data and may never be reported as results, which is a provenance rule rather than a matter of taste.

Phase 4 has you record the model tag, the date and the commit next to every run, and tags exist to make that cheap.

reproducibility

Somebody else, running your code on your data, gets your numbers. Four things buy it here: a local model, temperature 0, the dataset committed to the repository, and a test suite that cross-checks the metrics. It is a stronger claim than "I wrote down what I did" and a weaker one than "the finding is true".

Note the honest caveat under determinism: two runs at temperature 0 may still differ slightly, and Phase 4 has you count the disagreements rather than hide them.

blind second labelling

A second person labels some of your responses without seeing your labels first. The blinding is the whole point: a reader who can see that you wrote M will agree with M, and you will have measured politeness rather than clarity. Ten items is the minimum worth reporting on this project.

Phase 3, near the end, and it is a gate: if the second reader cannot reproduce your labels, the dataset gets fixed before anything is run.

inter-rater agreement

How often two independent labellers give the same label to the same item. Report the raw percentage first because everyone understands it, then Cohen's kappa because the raw percentage flatters you. Low agreement is not a fact about your reader: it means the category is not well defined, and a model cannot fairly be blamed for missing a distinction two humans cannot make.

Cohen's kappa

Agreement between two raters after subtracting the agreement chance alone would produce: \(\kappa = (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)\), where \(p_o\) is observed agreement and \(p_e\) is expected agreement. On a balanced two-category subset \(p_e = \tfrac12\), so the whole thing collapses to \(\kappa = 2p_o - 1\) and you can check any kappa in your head. Two raters agreeing 70% of the time on a coin-flip task have \(\kappa = 0.4\), which is the number telling the truth.

Phase 3 asks for \(\kappa \geq 0.6\), which on a balanced subset means agreeing on at least 80% of items. Kappa handles exactly two raters cited.

2 · Measurement

Every number in this study is arithmetic on one 2×2 table, with M as the positive class throughout:

$$\text{accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FN + FP + TN}, \qquad \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$$ $$\text{specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}} = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN}$$
Lab · which cells does this metric actually read?

The five metrics differ in exactly one way: which boxes of the table they are allowed to look at. Pick one and the cells it reads light up, the cells it ignores go grey. The readout gives the formula on a worked example and — the useful part — one sentence on what that metric is blind to. Every disagreement about which metric to report is really a disagreement about which blindness you can live with.

confusion matrix

The 2×2 table that holds a whole run: rows are the truth you labelled, columns are what the model said, and every item lands in exactly one of four boxes. The diagonal is agreement and the off-diagonal is error. Everything in Phase 5 is arithmetic on this table, so being able to fill one in by hand is worth more than being able to name any metric.

Drawn for you by G.matrix() on this site and by compute_confusion() in python/misconception/analyze.py.

true positive (TP)

Truly M, and the model said M: it caught a real misconception. The only cell that appears in the numerator of precision, recall and F1 alike, which is why all three are zero when it is zero.

false negative (FN)

Truly M, and the model said C: it missed one. A student held a belief like "rotating my password monthly keeps me safe", the tutor read it and said nothing, and the student leaves with a machine's silence as confirmation. This is the expensive error on this project, and the reason recall is the headline metric.

false positive (FP)

Truly C, and the model said M: a false alarm, a student corrected who was already right. Genuinely bad — enough of it and the student stops trusting the tool, which eventually costs you the true positives too — but nobody's account gets taken over because a tutor was over-cautious. Unequal costs between FN and FP are why one metric gets to lead.

true negative (TN)

Truly C, and the model said C: correctly left alone. The quiet cell. Accuracy and specificity read it; precision, recall and F1 never do, because none of them is about the correct answers.

accuracy

Of all forty items, the fraction the model got right: \((TP + TN)/n\). The metric everyone reaches for first and the least informative of the five, because it cannot tell six misses and three false alarms from three misses and six false alarms. It is only interpretable next to the majority-class baseline.

Report it, never lead with it, and never write "accuracy" when you mean precision or recall — that swap is the most common error in this whole project.

precision

Of the alarms the model raised, the fraction that were real: \(TP/(TP + FP)\). The denominator is everything the model called M. If the model raises no alarms at all the denominator is zero and precision is undefined, not zero — G.metrics returns null and G.pct prints undefined, deliberately, because printing 0.00 would claim every alarm was wrong.

The memory hook, worth carrying into Phase 5: precision is about the model's claims, recall is about the world's misconceptions.

recall

Of the misconceptions that were really there, the fraction the model caught: \(TP/(TP + FN)\). The denominator is everything that truly was M — twenty items, by construction, in this design. It is the headline metric here because a false negative is the error that actually costs something.

The pre-registered prediction is about this number: the aware condition should raise recall more than it raises precision.

specificity

Of the responses that were really correct, the fraction the model left alone: \(TN/(TN + FP)\). It is recall's mirror image, computed as though C were the positive class. Returned as spec by G.metrics.

Use it as a cross-check. Reading a result both ways round catches a swapped denominator within seconds.

F1

One number for when you want to talk about precision and recall together: their harmonic mean, which also equals \(2TP/(2TP + FP + FN)\). It ignores TN completely. F1 is the summary; recall is the headline; reporting F1 alone hides which of the two halves is carrying it.

harmonic mean

For two positive numbers, \(2ab/(a+b)\). It is never larger than the ordinary arithmetic mean, and equals it only when the two numbers are equal, so it is always pulled towards the smaller one. A classifier that flags every item has recall 100% and precision 50%: the arithmetic mean flatters it with 0.75, the harmonic mean gives 0.667.

That single property is the entire reason F1 uses it — a very high number cannot rescue a very low one.

majority-class baseline

The accuracy you get by ignoring the input entirely and always guessing whichever class is more common. On this balanced twenty-M, twenty-C design it is 50%; on a set of ninety M and ten C it would be 90%, and "90% accuracy" there would mean precisely nothing. Returned as majority by G.metrics.

Every accuracy you report is reported against this floor, in the same sentence. Balancing the dataset at 20/20 is what makes the floor a clean 50%, and is one of the better reasons to have balanced it.

confidence interval

A range of values consistent with the data you have, at a stated level. "95%" describes the procedure, not the number: intervals built this way capture the true value 95% of the time in repeated use. The interval is not decoration around the estimate — at n = 40 it is frequently the finding, because it is what turns "62.5%" into "somewhere between 46% and 77%".

Any page here that reports a number shows its interval. Use Wilson, never the textbook normal approximation.

Wilson score interval

The confidence interval for a proportion that this project uses everywhere, available as G.wilson(k, n). The reason is concrete: the textbook interval \(p \pm 1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) gives an upper bound of 1.018 at 38 correct out of 40 — an accuracy of 101.8%, which is not a thing that exists. Wilson gives 83.5% to 98.6% and never leaves the unit interval by construction. cited

Phase 1 and Phase 5 both use it; the metric tiles under every matrix on this site carry Wilson intervals.

McNemar's test

The significance test for two conditions run on the same items. It throws away every item the two conditions agreed on and looks only at the discordant pairs: \(b\), where the aware condition was right and the baseline wrong, and \(c\), the other way round. It then asks whether \(b\) and \(c\) are lopsided beyond what a coin flip would produce. cited

It is the right test here precisely because both conditions saw the same forty responses, which an unpaired test would ignore and thereby waste.

statistical significance

A result is called significant when it would be unlikely under the assumption that there is no real difference; the conventional threshold is a p-value below 0.05. Two things it does not mean. "Not significant" is not "no difference" — at this sample size it usually means not enough data. And significant is not the same as large: see effect size.

effect size

How big the difference is, in units a reader can picture, as opposed to how confident you are that it is not zero. "Recall rose from 55% to 75%" is an effect size, and it means four more misconceptions caught out of twenty. Report it first and the significance second; a p-value with no effect size attached tells the reader nothing they wanted to know.

sample size

The number of items, written \(n\). Here \(n = 40\): twenty M and twenty C. It is the single number that controls how wide every interval comes out, and it improves slowly — roughly, narrowing an interval by half takes four times the data.

Forty is a deliberate choice: enough to detect a large effect, honestly admitted as too few for a small one, and few enough that you can write all forty responses carefully by hand in Phase 3.

3 · Language models

language model

A program trained on a very large amount of text to predict the next token, and therefore to continue any text you give it. That is the whole mechanism: it is not looking anything up, it holds no beliefs, and it has no record of being right before. Everything it appears to know is a consequence of continuation being an extremely demanding thing to be good at.

Yours is llama3.2:3b, named once in config.py as MODEL_NAME.

parameter

One of the numbers learned during training. The :3b in llama3.2:3b means three billion of them. More parameters generally means more capable, slower, and hungrier for memory — the reason a 7B model on an 8 GB laptop is not slightly slower than a 3B one but several times slower, because it stops fitting and the machine starts swapping to disk.

token

The chunk of text a model actually reads and writes: usually a common word, or a piece of a rarer one, or a punctuation mark. Models do not see letters and they do not see whole sentences. MAX_TOKENS = 200 in config.py caps how long a reply is allowed to be, which is plenty for one verdict and one sentence of reason.

prompt

Everything you send to the model in one request: the instructions, the student response, the required output format, and — in one condition only — the catalogue. In this study the prompt is the independent variable, so the two prompts are built in python/misconception/prompts.py and read side by side rather than tweaked by feel.

system prompt

In many chat APIs, a separate slot for standing instructions — who the model is, what rules it follows — sent ahead of the user's message. Worth knowing because you will read about it everywhere. This project does not use one: the request in llm_client.py posts a single prompt field, so role, catalogue, response and output format all live in one string, and the two conditions differ in exactly one visible place.

temperature

How much randomness the model uses when choosing each next token. At 1.0 it samples freely and gives a different answer every time; at 0 it takes the most likely option at every step. TEMPERATURE = 0.0 here, not because deterministic output is better output but because you are running a comparison: it removes one source of variation so that the one you introduced on purpose is what is left.

determinism

Same input, same output, every time. Temperature 0 aims at it and does not guarantee it: floating-point addition is not associative, so the order a machine happens to sum things in can flip a near-tie between two candidate tokens, and library versions, batching and hardware all change that order.

Phase 4 has you run the whole thing twice and count the items where the two runs disagree. That count is what tells a reader how much of any difference between conditions could be run-to-run noise. observed

inference

Running a trained model to get an output, as opposed to training it. Nothing is learned during inference: your forty responses do not change the model, and running them in a different order does not change any individual verdict. Every call in this project is inference, which is part of why it fits on a laptop.

local model

A model whose weights are on your own disk and which runs on your own processor, rather than a service you send text to over the internet. Free, no API key, no rate limit, no student writing leaving the machine — and, importantly for this study, it does not change underneath you between March and June the way a hosted model can.

This is the argument for using a deliberately small model, and it is worth being able to give: see the Ollama page.

quantisation

Storing a model's parameters at lower numerical precision — say four bits each instead of sixteen — so the file is smaller and the arithmetic faster, at some cost in quality. It is why a three-billion-parameter model is a 2.0 GB download rather than the six or so gigabytes sixteen-bit weights would need.

You do not have to choose it: the builds Ollama pulls by default are already quantised. Knowing the word explains the download size.

context window

The maximum number of tokens a model can hold in view at once — prompt and reply together. Text beyond it is not summarised, it is simply not there. Relevant here because the aware prompt is much longer than the baseline one: a long list of ten misconceptions plus a student response is still small for a modern model, but it is the direction in which things break.

hallucination

Fluent, confident output that is simply not true. The failure mode to watch for in this study is a model that quotes back a misconception from the catalogue which the student never expressed — the reason each row records the model's one-sentence reason as well as its verdict. A right answer for an invented reason is still worth noticing.

parsing

Pulling a structured value out of unstructured text — here, extracting a single letter from whatever the model wrote. parse_verdict() in llm_client.py tries four strategies in order: the requested VERDICT: M format, a bare M or C, keyword sniffing, and then giving up.

Giving up is a design decision, not a gap: see unparseable reply.

unparseable reply

A reply from which no verdict can be extracted, recorded as ? rather than guessed. Quietly defaulting those to C would make your numbers look better than they are, so the code refuses to. The count is a real finding about small-model reliability: "the 3B model produced unparseable output on 12% of items" belongs in your results.

If the count is high, blunt the output-format instruction in prompts.py first, and move up a model size second — but write the number down before you change anything.

4 · The tools

Ollama

The program that runs a local model for you and exposes it as a small web server on your own machine. It is not an app you type questions into. Three separate things have to be right — the program installed, the server running, the model downloaded — and collapsing them into one is where nearly all of the confusion on that step comes from.

Setup step 2, which is the page most likely to cost you a week if you skim it.

localhost

A name that means "this machine" — the computer talking to itself over the network without the network ever being involved. It always resolves to the address 127.0.0.1. When an error says it could not reach http://localhost:11434, it is saying exactly one thing: I knocked on that door and nobody answered.

port

A numbered door on a machine, so that many programs can listen at the same address without colliding. Ollama's is 11434. This is why ollama serve can fail with "address already in use": something is already listening at that door, and that something is Ollama. That error means the thing you wanted has already happened.

HTTP request

The message format the web runs on: a method, an address, and a body. Your Python sends a POST to http://localhost:11434/api/generate with the prompt in the body, and gets text back. Every "AI integration" you will ever read about is, underneath, a POST like this one — worth knowing the first time somebody tries to sell you a framework for doing it.

JSON

A plain-text format for data made of keys and values, readable by both people and programs. The request carries {"model": ..., "prompt": ..., "options": {"temperature": 0}} and the reply comes back as JSON with a response field. r.json().get("response", "") in llm_client.py is the line that pulls the text out.

server

A program that sits waiting for requests and answers them. It is a role, not a machine: here the server and the client are both your laptop. The practical consequence is that it has to be running whenever your script runs, which is a different condition from being installed.

model tag

The full name of a model, in two parts separated by a colon: llama3.2 is the family and :3b is the size. Whatever appears in the NAME column of ollama list must match MODEL_NAME in config.py character for character. Mismatched tags are the single most common cause of "model not found".

Record the exact tag in your write-up. It is part of the provenance of every number you report.

CSV

Comma-separated values: a plain text table, one record per line, with the column names on the first line. Boring on purpose — it opens in a spreadsheet, in a text editor, and in Python, and it is readable in twenty years. Your two data files, responses.csv and misconceptions.csv, are CSV, and so are the per-item predictions a run writes to python/results/.

pandas

The Python library for handling tables of data: reading a CSV, counting rows that satisfy a condition, grouping by a column. Installed by pip3 install -r requirements.txt. You use a small and well-signposted part of it in Phase 2.

DataFrame

pandas' table object: rows, named columns, and one type per column. df["truth"] == "M" gives a column of true and false, and summing that column counts the items — which is how compute_confusion() counts the four cells of the matrix without a single loop.

scikit-learn

The standard Python machine-learning library. This project uses almost none of it: it is here only so the test suite can compute the same metrics a second, independent way and check that your arithmetic agrees. Two independent implementations agreeing is a much stronger statement than one implementation looking right.

cross_check_with_sklearn() in analyze.py, and the tests in python/tests/test_pipeline.py.

matplotlib

The Python plotting library. It draws the confusion matrices and the condition comparison that analyze.py saves into python/results/ as image files. Those are the figures that go in your write-up, so they are produced by code rather than redrawn by hand — which means they update when the data does.

pytest

The test runner. pytest -q from the repository root executes everything in python/tests/ and prints a line of dots. A test is a small piece of code that asserts something must be true; here they check that the baseline prompt never leaks the catalogue, that the aware prompt contains every misconception, and that the metrics match scikit-learn.

Run it before every commit, and always before you tag.

virtual environment

A private folder holding one project's installed packages, so two projects that want different versions of the same library do not fight. You will meet the term constantly in Python documentation, usually as venv. This project deliberately does not require one — the setup page installs the four libraries with pip3 directly, because an extra step that has to be re-activated in every new terminal is a good way to lose a week.

.venv/ is nonetheless listed in .gitignore, so that if you do create one it never gets committed.

5 · Version control

repository

A folder that git is tracking, together with its entire history. "The repo" for this project means the folder containing python/, docs/ and requirements.txt — and every state it has been in since it was created. The history is the part that makes it a repository rather than a folder.

clone

To make a local copy of a repository, history included, with git clone. A clone is a full copy rather than a checkout: you can work, commit and read the whole history with the network switched off.

This is also the operation somebody else performs when they reproduce your study, which is why the dataset and the results you report get committed.

commit

A saved snapshot of the whole project at one moment, with a message saying what changed and why. Commits are cheap and permanent — you can always come back to one. The message is written for the person who reads it in three months, and that person is you.

Setup step 3. Commit at the end of every session, and always before you try something you are not sure about.

commit hash

The forty-character hexadecimal identifier of a commit, of which the first seven characters are normally enough to name it unambiguously. It is the most precise way to say which version of the code produced a number.

Your write-up cites the hash or the tag of the commit the real run was made from. That single string is most of what provenance means in practice.

tag

A readable name pinned permanently to one commit, such as phase-4-complete. It exists so a figure in your report can name the state of the repository that produced it without anyone having to read a hash aloud. Note that git push and git push --tags are separate acts.

Only tag on a green pytest -q. A tag is a promise that this state works, and a tag on a broken commit is worse than no tag because it is an assertion nobody checked.

.gitignore

A file listing paths git should not track: caches, editor settings, and in this project the contents of results/, so a half-finished run never gets committed by accident. The final run you actually report is added on purpose with git add -f, which makes committing your results a deliberate act rather than a side effect.

remote

A copy of the repository living somewhere other than your laptop, conventionally named origin and hosted on GitHub. It is what makes your work survive the laptop, and what lets a mentor read the code without you emailing a zip file.

push

Sending your commits to the remote. The sentence to keep: a commit that has not been pushed does not exist off your laptop. Everything committed since your last push is work a spilled drink can still take, and the amount grows every hour you do not push.

GitHub Pages

GitHub's free web hosting: point it at a folder in a repository and it serves those files as an ordinary website. The docs/ folder is the conventional choice, and this page is a file inside it — which is why the site is plain HTML with no build step, and why every link on it is relative.

continuous integration

A service that runs your tests automatically every time you push. This repository has one: .github/workflows/ci.yml starts a fresh Linux machine, clones the repo onto it, and runs pytest on two versions of Python. Nothing to turn on; a green tick or a red cross appears next to the commit.

Its real value is that it proves the project runs on a machine that is not yours. "It works on my laptop" is the most common wrong claim in software, and this is how you find out within sixty seconds instead of the night before a deadline.

6 · Which term is this describing?

Four descriptions with no names attached. Each one is a situation you can actually meet on this project, and naming it correctly is what lets you say what to do next.

Exercise 6.1 ★thinking

You run both conditions and every single one of your twenty M responses is caught by both. Recall is 100% in the baseline and 100% in the misconception-aware condition. Name what has happened, say why it ends this version of the study, and say what you do about it.

Solution

A ceiling effect. The measurement has hit its maximum in the baseline condition, so the intervention has nowhere to show an improvement: recall cannot exceed 100%, and any difference between conditions is invisible by construction.

Notice that this is a fact about your items, not about the model. Twenty M responses that every reader and every model catch are twenty responses written too obviously wrong — "I use the password password because nobody would guess something that simple" rather than "I use a twenty-four character passphrase, so length has me covered".

The fix is in Phase 3, not Phase 4: rewrite the M items so the misconception is embedded in a response that is otherwise sensible and confident. Then re-run the blind second labelling, because harder items are also items a second reader may label differently — you are trading ceiling risk against agreement, and you need to see both numbers.

Exercise 6.2 ★★arithmetic

Both conditions ran on the same forty responses. They gave the same verdict on 31 of them. Of the 9 they disagreed on, the misconception-aware condition was right on 7 and the baseline was right on 2. Name the test that is designed for exactly this situation, say why the paired structure matters, and — using the exact version of the test, which is a two-sided binomial test on the discordant pairs — work out whether 7 against 2 is significant at the conventional 0.05 threshold.

Solution

McNemar's test cited. It is built for two conditions measured on the same items, which is exactly this design: every one of the forty responses went through both prompts.

The paired structure matters because the 31 items both conditions agreed on carry no information about which condition is better — they were easy, or they were hard, for both. An unpaired test would spend its power on those 31 and dilute the signal. McNemar throws them away and looks only at the discordant pairs, here \(b = 7\) and \(c = 2\).

The arithmetic. Under the null hypothesis that the two conditions are equally good, each of the 9 discordant items is a coin flip, so the number won by the aware condition is binomial with \(n = 9\) and \(p = \tfrac12\). The two-sided p-value is twice the probability of a split at least as lopsided as 7–2:

\(P(X \geq 7) = \dfrac{\binom{9}{7} + \binom{9}{8} + \binom{9}{9}}{2^9} = \dfrac{36 + 9 + 1}{512} = \dfrac{46}{512} = 0.0898\)

and so \(p = 2 \times 0.0898 = 0.180\). Not significant at 0.05.

The lesson is the one worth carrying: 7 against 2 looks lopsided, and with only 9 discordant pairs it is entirely ordinary — a fair coin produces a split this uneven or worse about one time in five. The honest sentence is "the aware condition won 7 of the 9 items the two conditions disagreed on, which at this sample size is consistent with chance". That is a finding, and it is the finding a pilot at n = 40 is likely to produce.

Exercise 6.3 ★★by hand

A draft of your results says: "The misconception-aware condition reached an accuracy of 62.5%, showing that the model performs well above chance." Two terms from this glossary are missing from that sentence, and each one, once supplied, changes what the sentence is allowed to claim. Name both, and rewrite the sentence.

Solution

The majority-class baseline and the confidence interval.

The floor. Your dataset is twenty M and twenty C, so a classifier that ignores the response entirely and answers M every time scores 20/40 = 50%. "Chance" is not 0%; it is 50%. So 62.5% is 12.5 percentage points above the floor, not "well above chance".

The interval. 62.5% of 40 is 25 correct. The 95% Wilson interval for 25/40 runs from 47.0% to 75.8%, which contains 50%. So this accuracy is consistent with a model doing nothing at all, and the word "showing" is not available to you.

A defensible rewrite: "The misconception-aware condition reached an accuracy of 62.5% (25/40, 95% Wilson interval 47.0–75.8%), against a majority-class floor of 50%. The interval includes the floor, so this run does not establish that the model performs above chance-level guessing."

Two sentences, no rhetoric, and nothing in it can be taken away from you later. Note also that accuracy is the wrong headline regardless: it is blind to which errors were made, and recall is the number this design is about.

Exercise 6.4 ★★writing

You finish the run. Recall barely moves, but F1 is up by five points, so you lead the write-up with F1. Separately, three responses were awkward to label, so you drop them and report on 37 items. Neither decision felt dishonest at the time. Name what you have done, name the practice that would have prevented it, and say what you can still do now that the data exists.

Solution

You have spent researcher degrees of freedom: choices left open until after the data arrived, each made in the direction that helped. Nobody has to intend anything dishonest for this to manufacture a result — twenty reasonable-looking decisions is plenty, and the reason it is dangerous is precisely that each one is defensible on its own.

The practice that prevents it is pre-registration: recall is named as the primary metric, and the rule for dropping an item is written down, both before the run. Your Phase 1 prediction plus the commit date is a small but real version of this.

What you can still do, now that the data exists. Nothing is lost — you simply report in the order you promised and label the rest as exploratory:

  • Lead with recall on all 40 items, including the three awkward ones, because that is the analysis you committed to. If recall barely moved, say so: "no detectable difference at this sample size" is a finding.
  • Report the F1 improvement immediately afterwards, marked as exploratory, and say what would test it properly — a fresh set of items, which is a sentence about future work, not an excuse.
  • Report the three awkward items rather than deleting them. Why they were awkward is real evidence about your ground truth, and reporting both the 40-item and 37-item numbers costs one line and settles the question for the reader.

The failed project is not the one with a small effect. It is the one where the reader cannot tell which decisions were made before the data and which after.
