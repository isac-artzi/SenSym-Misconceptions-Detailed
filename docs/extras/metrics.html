The metrics playground

Five metrics, one 2×2 table, and three labs that show what each of them hides.

Every number this project reports comes out of a single four-cell table. This page is the place to come back to when you have forgotten which one precision is, and the page to point a reader at when they ask what your headline number means. It is mostly sliders: read a paragraph, then go and break something in the lab underneath it.

By the end of this page you can

  • State which class is the positive class here, and say why the whole page depends on it.
  • Compute accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and specificity from a 2×2 table by hand.
  • Spot the always-says-M trap from accuracy alone, and say why 50% is worth nothing.
  • Explain why precision can be undefined, and why writing 0 there is a lie.
  • Say what the harmonic mean in F1 is punishing, and show it with two numbers.
  • Read a Wilson interval and state how wide the fog is at n = 40.
  • Say how many items would be needed to resolve a 10-point difference, and what to do about the fact that you have forty.

1 · The one table everything comes from

Forty responses go into the model. For each one there are two facts: what the response really was, which you decided when you wrote it, and what the model said. Two facts with two values each gives four possible combinations, and every metric on this page is some ratio of those four counts. There is nothing else in the machine.

The positive class is M

M means "this response contains a misconception". C means "this response is correct". M is the positive class, so "true positive" means the model correctly said a misconception was present.

This is a choice, not a law, and it is the choice that makes precision and recall mean what they mean. Flip it — call C positive — and every number on this page changes. A metric quoted without its positive class is not a result; it is a number. Say "recall with respect to M" the first time it appears in anything you write.

An illustrative table, used throughout this page so the arithmetic is always checkable. It is not a result: no number on this page comes from a real run.

Read the four cells as sentences. TP = 14: fourteen responses really did contain a misconception and the model said so. FN = 6: six really did and the model said nothing — six students walked away still believing that rotating a password monthly protects them. FP = 3: three responses were fine and got corrected anyway. TN = 17: seventeen were fine and were left alone.

The two error cells are not interchangeable. A false negative is a student who keeps a wrong belief about security. A false positive is a student who is told off for being right. Both are bad; only one of them ends with somebody's account taken. That asymmetry is the entire reason recall is this project's headline metric, and it is a design decision you should be able to defend out loud.

The five definitions, once, properly:

$$\text{accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+FN+FP+TN}\qquad \text{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}\qquad \text{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$ $$F_1=\frac{2\cdot\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}}\qquad \text{specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}$$

Two of those are worth reading aloud until they stick. Recall divides by the column of things that were really there — of the misconceptions present, what fraction did the model catch. Precision divides by the row of alarms it raised — of the times it shouted, how often was it right. Recall is about what you missed; precision is about whether to trust the shouting. People swap them constantly, including in published papers.

Undefined is not zero

Click always says C. TP = 0 and FP = 0, so precision is 0/0. That is undefined, not zero. G.metrics returns null and G.pct prints undefined on purpose: printing 0.0% would assert that every alarm the model raised was wrong, which is a different claim and a false one, because it raised none.

F1 is undefined here too, because it is built from precision. In prose write "precision is undefined: the model raised no alarms", never "precision was 0". This distinction is not pedantry. A pipeline that silently turns undefined into zero will average it into a mean and report a number that describes nothing.

4 · Lab 2 · Why F1 is a harmonic mean

F1 is the one metric that is not a simple count divided by another count. It is a mean of precision and recall — but the harmonic mean, not the ordinary one you learned in school. That choice is doing real work, and the always-says-M preset shows exactly what.

The always-says-M classifier, in two means

Precision 0.500, recall 1.000. The arithmetic mean is $(0.500 + 1.000)/2 = 0.750$, which sounds like a respectable result for a model that answers M to every item without reading it.

The harmonic mean, which is what F1 is, gives

$$F_1=\frac{2\times 0.500\times 1.000}{0.500+1.000}=\frac{1.000}{1.500}=0.667$$

Still generous, but 0.083 lower, and the gap grows the more lopsided the pair becomes. The harmonic mean is punishing imbalance: it is pulled towards the smaller of the two numbers, so a spectacular recall cannot buy its way out of a poor precision.

The harmonic mean never exceeds the arithmetic mean proved

For positive $a$ and $b$, write $A=\dfrac{a+b}{2}$ and $H=\dfrac{2ab}{a+b}$. Then

$$A-H=\frac{(a+b)^2-4ab}{2(a+b)}=\frac{(a-b)^2}{2(a+b)}\;\ge\;0$$

because a square is never negative and $a+b>0$. Equality holds exactly when $a=b$. So F1 sits at or below the arithmetic mean always, and the shortfall is governed by $(a-b)^2$ — the square of the gap between precision and recall. Doubling the imbalance quadruples the penalty. ∎

Lab 2 · The gap between the two means

Recall is held fixed at whatever the first slider says; the curves show F1 and the arithmetic mean as precision sweeps from bad to perfect. Set recall to 100% and precision to 50% to reproduce the always-says-M numbers. Then drag precision down towards zero and watch the two curves separate: that widening gap is the penalty for being good at one thing and bad at the other. Set precision equal to recall and the gap closes to nothing.

One more fact about F1 that the algebra hides. Substituting the definitions and simplifying gives

$$F_1=\frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP+FP+FN}$$

which contains no $TN$ at all. F1 is completely blind to true negatives. Add a thousand correct responses that the model correctly ignores and F1 does not move by a hair, while accuracy climbs towards 100%. That is a feature when the positive class is the rare and expensive one — and a reason F1 and accuracy can disagree violently about the same table.

5 · Lab 3 · How wide is the fog at n = 40

This is the most important picture on the site. Every number you report is a proportion measured on forty items, and a proportion measured on forty items is uncertain. Not uncertain in a hand-waving way: uncertain by a specific, computable amount, and that amount is much larger than most people's intuition.

The interval used here is the Wilson score interval, not the textbook "estimate ± 1.96 standard errors" normal approximation. The reason is concrete: at 38 out of 40 the normal approximation gives 88.2% to 101.8%, and an accuracy of 101.8% is not a thing that exists. Wilson gives 83.5% to 98.6% and never leaves the interval [0, 1].

Lab 3 · The shrinking band

Suppose your observed accuracy comes out at exactly the value in the dropdown. The shaded band is the 95% Wilson interval you would report at each sample size; the dashed vertical line is n = 40, where this study actually lives. Drag the slider and notice how slowly the fog clears — the band narrows like one over the square root of n, so halving the width costs four times the data.

What this means for your write-up

At n = 40 with an observed accuracy of 77.5%, the 95% interval runs from 62.5% to 87.7% — 25 percentage points wide. If the misconception-aware condition scores ten points above baseline, that difference sits comfortably inside both intervals, and you cannot claim it as established from these data alone.

This is not a failure of the project. It is the correct reading of forty items, and stating it plainly is what makes the rest of the write-up trustworthy. Report the effect, report the interval, describe the direction, and call it a pilot. A confident claim from a 25-point-wide interval is the single easiest way for a reader to stop believing everything else you wrote.

6 · Quick reference

Every metric on one screen. The last column is the one to read before you quote a number.

MetricFormulaWhat it answersWhen it misleads
accuracy $\dfrac{TP+TN}{n}$ Of all forty responses, what fraction did the model get right? Whenever the classes are unbalanced, and even when balanced: always-says-M scores 50% here while knowing nothing. Never quote it without the baseline.
precision $\dfrac{TP}{TP+FP}$ Of the alarms the model raised, what fraction were real misconceptions? A model that raises one alarm and gets it right scores 100%. Undefined when it raises none. Always pair it with recall.
recall
the headline		 $\dfrac{TP}{TP+FN}$ Of the misconceptions that were really there, what fraction did the model catch? A model that answers M to everything scores 100%. Recall alone cannot be the whole report — precision is what stops the always-says-M cheat.
F1 $\dfrac{2PR}{P+R}=\dfrac{2\,TP}{2\,TP+FP+FN}$ One number summarising precision and recall together, weighted towards the worse of the two. It hides which of the two is weak, and it ignores $TN$ entirely. It is not a proportion of anything, so a Wilson interval does not apply to it.
specificity $\dfrac{TN}{TN+FP}$ Of the responses that were actually correct, what fraction did the model leave alone? A model that answers C to everything scores 100%. It is recall's mirror image and is free to a silent classifier.
majority-class baseline $\dfrac{\max(TP+FN,\;FP+TN)}{n}$ What accuracy do you get for free by always guessing the bigger class? It is 50% here by construction, so it looks harmless and gets forgotten — which is exactly how a 55% accuracy gets reported as a success.
false negatives $FN$ How many students kept a wrong belief about security? It is a raw count, so it is only comparable between runs of the same size. Here every run is forty items, so it is directly comparable — and it is the number that actually matters.
Check this page against the code

Nothing here is a special web version of the arithmetic. The four headline formulas live in compute_metrics() in python/misconception/analyze.py, and python/tests/test_pipeline.py cross-checks them against scikit-learn. Run the pipeline on demo data and compare its printed metrics against Lab 1 with the same four counts:

$ cd python
$ python3 misconception/run_experiment.py --mock --sample
$ python3 misconception/analyze.py
$ pytest ../ -q

Two differences to know about, because they will bite you. analyze.py computes only accuracy, precision, recall and F1 — specificity and the majority-class baseline are not in it, so those you work out yourself from the printed matrix. And where this page prints undefined, compute_metrics() currently returns 0.0 for a zero denominator, guarded by if (tp + fp) else 0.0.

So if a run ever prints precision=0.0, check the matrix before you write it down: if TP and FP are both zero, the honest entry is undefined, not zero. Anything produced by --mock or --sample is demo data — it proves the plumbing works and never goes in the write-up.

7 · Five words, defined properly

positive class

The one of the two labels that precision, recall and F1 are computed about. Here it is M. "True positive" means the model said M and M was correct; "false negative" means M was correct and the model said C. Swapping which class is positive gives a completely different set of numbers from the same table, which is why the choice is stated every time and never assumed.

majority-class baseline

The accuracy obtained by ignoring the input entirely and always answering with whichever class is more common. Here the design fixes twenty M and twenty C, so the baseline is 50% by construction. It is the floor a result has to clear before it means anything: an accuracy of 52% is not "slightly good", it is indistinguishable from a model that never looks at the response.

confidence interval

A range of values for the underlying rate that are consistent with what you observed. "95%" describes the procedure, not this particular interval: if you repeated the whole study many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true rate. This project uses the Wilson score interval, G.wilson(k, n), because the normal approximation is badly behaved at small n and near 0 or 1 — it produces intervals running past 100%, which is visibly wrong.

ceiling effect

When a measure is already so close to its maximum that an improvement has nowhere to go. If the baseline prompt already scores 95% recall, the misconception-aware prompt can gain at most five points, and the study cannot detect the improvement it was designed to look for — not because the improvement is absent, but because the ruler has run out. This is a real risk in Phase 3: writing misconception responses that are too obviously wrong drives the baseline to the ceiling and destroys the experiment before the model is ever run. The fix is in the dataset, not the statistics.

harmonic mean

For two positive numbers, $H = \dfrac{2ab}{a+b}$ — equivalently, the reciprocal of the average of the reciprocals. It is always at or below the arithmetic mean, by exactly $(a-b)^2 / (2(a+b))$, and it is dragged towards the smaller input. F1 uses it so that a model cannot score well by being excellent at precision and hopeless at recall, or the reverse.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 8.1 ★arithmetic

A run gives $TP = 13$, $FN = 7$, $FP = 2$, $TN = 18$. Compute accuracy, precision, recall, F1, specificity and the majority-class baseline. Show every division. Then check your answers by setting the sliders in Lab 1.

Solution

First check the table is legal: $13 + 7 + 2 + 18 = 40$, and the positive column $TP + FN = 20$ with the negative column $FP + TN = 20$, as the design requires.

accuracy $= (13 + 18)/40 = 31/40 = 0.775$, so 77.5%.

precision $= 13/(13 + 2) = 13/15 = 0.8667$, so 86.7%. Of the fifteen alarms it raised, thirteen were real.

recall $= 13/(13 + 7) = 13/20 = 0.650$, so 65.0%. Of the twenty misconceptions present, it caught thirteen.

F1: use the short form $2\,TP/(2\,TP + FP + FN) = 26/(26 + 2 + 7) = 26/35 = 0.7429$, so 74.3%. The long way agrees: $2 \times 0.8667 \times 0.650 / (0.8667 + 0.650) = 1.1267 / 1.5167 = 0.7429$.

specificity $= 18/(18 + 2) = 18/20 = 0.900$, so 90.0%.

baseline $= \max(20, 20)/40 = 0.500$, so 50.0%.

The shape to notice: precision 86.7% well above recall 65.0% means this model is cautious. It is right when it speaks and silent about seven misconceptions. In this project those seven are the expensive cell, so despite the healthy-looking accuracy this run would be reported as a recall problem.

Exercise 8.2 ★by hand

A classmate builds a "detector" that outputs M for every response without reading it. On your forty items it gets $TP = 20$, $FN = 0$, $FP = 20$, $TN = 0$. Compute all five metrics. Which of them make it look good, and what is the one sentence that demolishes the claim?

Solution

accuracy $= (20 + 0)/40 = 0.500$ — 50.0%.
precision $= 20/(20 + 20) = 20/40 = 0.500$ — 50.0%.
recall $= 20/(20 + 0) = 20/20 = 1.000$ — 100.0%.
F1 $= 2 \times 20/(40 + 20 + 0) = 40/60 = 0.6667$ — 66.7%.
specificity $= 0/(0 + 20) = 0.000$ — 0.0%.

Recall makes it look perfect, and F1 at 66.7% still sounds like a passing grade. Accuracy sits exactly on the 50% majority-class baseline, and specificity is a flat zero: it never once correctly left a good response alone.

The sentence: "It answers M to every input, so its accuracy equals the majority-class baseline of 50% and its specificity is 0% — it has not learned anything, it has learned to shout."

This is why the write-up reports recall and precision and the baseline together. Recall is the headline because missed misconceptions are the expensive error, but a headline metric that can be maxed out by a constant output is only safe when it is quoted beside the metric that catches the cheat.

Exercise 8.3 ★★arithmetic

The opposite failure: the model answers C to all forty items, giving $TP = 0$, $FN = 20$, $FP = 0$, $TN = 20$. Compute what you can. One metric cannot be computed — which, why, and what exactly do you write in the results table instead?

Solution

accuracy $= (0 + 20)/40 = 0.500$ — 50.0%.
recall $= 0/(0 + 20) = 0/20 = 0.000$ — 0.0%. This is zero: there were twenty misconceptions to catch and it caught none.
specificity $= 20/(20 + 0) = 1.000$ — 100.0%, which is free and meaningless.
precision $= 0/(0 + 0) = 0/0$ — undefined.

Precision asks "of the alarms you raised, what fraction were right?" This model raised no alarms, so the question has no answer. Zero would be the answer to a different question — "you raised alarms and every one was wrong" — which is false here. F1 is undefined too, because it is built from precision.

In the results table write undefined or — with a footnote: "precision and F1 are undefined: the model predicted M on zero items." G.metrics returns null for both and G.pct prints undefined, deliberately, so that the distinction survives all the way from the arithmetic to the page.

Note also that recall 0% and specificity 100% arrive together. A metric that is free to a constant classifier is not evidence of anything, and this pair is the cleanest demonstration of it on the whole page.

Exercise 8.4 ★★by hand

Model A has precision 0.90 and recall 0.50. Model B has precision 0.70 and recall 0.70. Their arithmetic means are identical. Compute F1 for each, then use the identity $A - H = (a-b)^2 / (2(a+b))$ to predict both gaps before you compute them, and confirm.

Solution

Both arithmetic means are $0.700$: $(0.90 + 0.50)/2 = 0.700$ and $(0.70 + 0.70)/2 = 0.700$.

Model A: $F_1 = 2 \times 0.90 \times 0.50 / (0.90 + 0.50) = 0.900 / 1.400 = 0.6429$.

Model B: $F_1 = 2 \times 0.70 \times 0.70 / (0.70 + 0.70) = 0.980 / 1.400 = 0.700$.

Predicted gaps from the identity. For A: $(0.90 - 0.50)^2 / (2 \times 1.400) = 0.160 / 2.800 = 0.0571$, and indeed $0.700 - 0.6429 = 0.0571$. For B: $(0.70 - 0.70)^2 / (2 \times 1.400) = 0$, and indeed $0.700 - 0.700 = 0$.

So F1 ranks B above A by 5.7 points even though the two models have the same average performance. That is the harmonic mean doing its job: A's balance is worse, and the penalty scales with the square of the imbalance.

For this project the ranking also matches what you actually care about. Model A misses half the misconceptions; Model B misses 30% of them. Since a missed misconception is the error with a real cost, the summary metric preferring B is the summary metric agreeing with the study design.

Exercise 8.5 ★★arithmetic

The baseline condition gets 27 of 40 right; the misconception_aware condition gets 31 of 40. Compute both 95% Wilson intervals using

$$\left[\frac{c-s}{d},\;\frac{c+s}{d}\right],\quad d = 1+\frac{z^2}{n},\quad c = \hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n},\quad s = z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}+\frac{z^2}{4n^2}}$$

with $z = 1.96$, $n = 40$. Then say precisely what you may and may not claim.

Solution

Both share $z^2 = 3.8416$, $d = 1 + 3.8416/40 = 1.09604$, and $z^2/(4n^2) = 3.8416/6400 = 0.00060$.

misconception_aware, 31/40, $\hat p = 0.775$.
$c = 0.775 + 3.8416/80 = 0.775 + 0.04802 = 0.82302$.
$s = 1.96\sqrt{0.775 \times 0.225/40 + 0.00060} = 1.96\sqrt{0.004359 + 0.000600} = 1.96 \times 0.07042 = 0.13803$.
Interval $= [(0.82302 - 0.13803)/1.09604,\; (0.82302 + 0.13803)/1.09604] = [0.625,\; 0.877]$ — 62.5% to 87.7%.

baseline, 27/40, $\hat p = 0.675$.
$c = 0.675 + 0.04802 = 0.72302$.
$s = 1.96\sqrt{0.675 \times 0.325/40 + 0.00060} = 1.96\sqrt{0.005484 + 0.000600} = 1.96 \times 0.07800 = 0.15289$.
Interval $= [(0.72302 - 0.15289)/1.09604,\; (0.72302 + 0.15289)/1.09604] = [0.520,\; 0.799]$ — 52.0% to 79.9%.

What you may claim. The observed difference is 10.0 percentage points in favour of the misconception-aware prompt, and the direction is the one the hypothesis predicted. Both point estimates are above the 50% majority-class baseline.

What you may not claim. The intervals overlap across almost their whole length — 62.5% to 79.9% is common to both. Forty items cannot separate these two conditions. Write "the misconception-aware prompt scored 10 points higher, but with n = 40 the 95% intervals overlap substantially and this pilot cannot establish the difference", and let the reader see the intervals. That sentence is worth more than an overstated one, because it is the sentence a reader can check.

Exercise 8.6 ★★★thinking

Use Lab 3 to find the sample size at which a 95% interval around an observed accuracy of 77.5% is narrower than 10 percentage points. Compare it with 40. Then answer the real question: given that forty is what you have, name three things you can do that make the study more informative without more data, and one thing you must not do.

Solution

At 77.5% observed accuracy the interval first drops below 10 points wide at n = 266, where it runs about 72.1% to 82.1%. At n = 40 it is 62.5% to 87.7%, 25.2 points wide. So resolving a 10-point difference would need roughly 6.7 times the dataset — about 226 further hand-written responses. At the rate good items get written, that is not this year's project.

Three things that help without more data:

1. Use the same forty responses in both conditions. The two prompts are run over an identical dataset, so the comparison is paired: every item acts as its own control, and the item-to-item variation that dominates the interval above partly cancels. The quantity to report is which items changed verdict between conditions and in which direction, not just two separate accuracies. For paired binary outcomes the standard test is McNemar's test cited, which uses only the disagreement counts; if you want it, it is one function call and needs the per-item results the pipeline already writes.

2. Report the error you care about as a count. "Missed misconceptions fell from 13 to 6 out of 20" is concrete, checkable, and does not pretend to more precision than it has. Counts are honest in a way that a percentage with a hidden interval is not.

3. Look at which items moved. Forty items is a poor sample and an excellent set of cases. If every newly-caught response involves the MFA misconception and none involve the rotation one, that is a finding about what the catalogue supplies, and it does not depend on the interval at all. Qualitative patterns survive small n; precise effect sizes do not.

And the thing you must not do: keep adjusting the prompt, rerunning, and reporting the best result. With intervals this wide, several reruns will produce a flattering number by chance alone, and choosing it turns noise into a claim. Decide the prompts and the metrics before the run, run once, and report what happened — including a rerun that came out differently at temperature 0, which is expected and belongs in the write-up rather than in the bin.

Going deeper (optional)

Three things that sit just past the edge of this page and are worth knowing exist.

F1 is not the only summary. The general form is $F_\beta = (1+\beta^2)PR / (\beta^2 P + R)$, which weights recall $\beta$ times as heavily as precision. Since this project holds that a missed misconception costs more than a false alarm, $F_2$ is arguably the more honest summary here than $F_1$. Reporting both, and saying why, is a stronger methods section than picking one silently.

The four counts are not four free numbers. The design fixes twenty M and twenty C, so $TP + FN = 20$ and $FP + TN = 20$ always: the table has exactly two degrees of freedom. That is why Lab 1 lets you break the constraint and the confusion-matrix lab in Phase 1 does not. Breaking it on purpose is how you see what the constraint was buying you.

Intervals are not the same as tests. Two overlapping intervals do not prove there is no difference; they say this sample cannot resolve one. Absence of evidence is being reported, not evidence of absence, and the difference between those two sentences is most of what a careful results section is made of.
