Analysis, and writing it honestly

Four files in python/results/, one interval that swallows most of them, and the sentences that survive.

This is the phase where the project becomes either a real piece of research or an overclaim. The arithmetic is easy — you already did all of it in Phase 1. The hard part is looking at a difference you were hoping for, computing its interval, and writing the sentence that the interval actually supports rather than the one you wanted. That sentence is the whole deliverable. Everything before this phase was building the machine that produces it.

By the end of this phase you can

  • Say exactly what analyze.py writes into python/results/, and read each file.
  • Read a confusion matrix by looking at the false-negative cell first, and say why that cell.
  • Compute a Wilson interval by hand and explain why it dominates every conclusion at n = 40.
  • State the comparison that matters — the difference in recall — and put an interval on the difference rather than on the two numbers separately.
  • Count the paired flips by hand and say what McNemar's test adds on top of them.
  • Name the five standard ways of fooling yourself, and say which one you were most tempted by.
  • Write Methods, Results and Limitations paragraphs that a hostile reader would believe — including where the pre-registered decoy condition goes, whether or not you ran it.
Every number on this page is a worked illustration

None of the matrices, percentages or intervals below came from your run, because your run has not happened on this page. They are arithmetic examples chosen to make a point. The same applies with more force to anything produced by --mock or --sample: those exist to prove the pipeline works and no number derived from them may appear in your write-up as a result. When your real numbers arrive, redo every calculation here with them.

1 · What analyze.py actually produces

Two commands, in this order. The first talks to the model and writes raw predictions; the second reads those and does arithmetic on them. They are separate on purpose: the expensive, fragile step happens once, and you can rerun the analysis a hundred times without waiting for the model again.

Run these 
$ cd python
$ python3 misconception/run_experiment.py
$ python3 misconception/analyze.py
$ pytest ../ -q

Add --mock --sample to either of the first two to rehearse without a model and without your own data. Rehearse as much as you like; report none of it.

Add --decoy to the run if you are also running the pre-registered placebo from Phase 1. It appends a third pass over the same forty items and writes results/predictions_decoy.csv; section 5 is what to do with it.

$ python3 misconception/run_experiment.py --decoy

analyze.py reads two files it did not create — results/predictions_baseline.csv and results/predictions_misconception_aware.csv, one row per response per condition, with columns response_id, misconception_id, response_text, ground_truth, prediction, model_reason, raw_reply and condition. It looks for a third, results/predictions_decoy.csv, and uses it if it is there. It writes three:

results/metrics.csv — one row per condition, ten columns: condition, TP, FP, FN, TN, unparseable, accuracy, precision, recall, f1. This is the table that goes in your write-up, and it is the only one of the three outputs you should quote numbers from. Note what is in it and what is not. It has the four counts, so a reader can recompute every metric and check you. It has unparseable, so items the parser could not read are visible rather than silently gone. And it has no intervals, because the script does not compute them: adding those is your job, by hand, and section 3 is how.

results/confusion_matrices.png — one 2×2 grid per condition, side by side, drawn by plot_confusion_matrices(). The layout is [[TP, FN], [FP, TN]], so the top-right cell is the misses.

results/comparison.png — a grouped bar chart of accuracy, precision, recall and F1 with one bar per condition per metric, drawn by plot_comparison(), with the y-axis fixed to 0–1.15 and the value printed above each bar. This is your Figure 1. It has no error bars, which is exactly the trap section 3 is about: eight bars of different heights are very persuasive and, at n = 40, mostly noise.

How the decoy gets into all three, without you doing anything

main() in analyze.py loops over config.ALL_CONDITIONS — baseline, misconception_aware, decoy — and for each one looks for results/predictions_<condition>.csv. If the file is missing it stops with an error, except for the decoy, which it skips with a bare continue. So: run with --decoy and you get a third row in metrics.csv, a third 2×2 grid in confusion_matrices.png and a third bar in each group of comparison.png, automatically. Run without it and nothing breaks and nothing is mentioned. Running the placebo is optional by design.

The silence cuts both ways, which is the part to watch. Nothing on your screen will remind you that the decoy is missing, so if you meant to run it and forgot, the only place that shows up is the row count in metrics.csv. Check it. And if you genuinely did not run it, that fact has to be carried by hand into Limitations, because no file will carry it for you.

analyze.py also prints to the terminal. Two lines per condition — the four counts with the unparseable count, then the four metrics — and then a cross-check line from cross_check_with_sklearn(). That line should say MATCH ✓. If it says MISMATCH ✗, your TODO 5 counts disagree with scikit-learn and the numbers in metrics.csv are wrong; fix that before reading anything else. Checking your own implementation against a trusted library is a habit worth keeping, and when they disagree it is almost always you.

=== baseline === TP=11 FP=4 FN=9 TN=16 (unparseable: 0) accuracy=0.675 precision=0.733 recall=0.55 f1=0.629 scikit-learn cross-check: MATCH ✓

2 · Reading your own confusion matrices

You get one matrix per condition, and the temptation is to read them the way you read a scoreboard — glance at the diagonal, see which is bigger, move on. Do the opposite. Look at the false-negative cell first, in both matrices, before you look at anything else.

The reason is the argument from Phase 1: the two off-diagonal cells are both errors and they do not cost the same. A false positive is a tutor correcting a student who was already right — irritating, trust-eroding, survivable. A false negative is a student who wrote down a wrong belief about passwords and had it read by a machine that said nothing. The belief survives, now with a machine's silence behind it. FN is the consequential cell, recall is the metric built on it, and recall is what your headline sentence is about. Reading FN first is a way of stopping yourself from telling a story about the diagonal.

A · baseline
B · misconception_aware

Read those two as one sentence each. Under the plain prompt, twenty responses really contained a misconception and the tutor walked past nine of them. Under the prompt with the catalogue, it walked past three. Then, and only then, look at the cost: false alarms rose from four to seven. Six silences became corrections; three students who were right got corrected anyway. That is the trade, stated in students rather than in decimals, and it is a far better opening line than "F1 improved from 0.629 to 0.773".

Three more things to do with the matrices before you write anything.

Check the row totals. TP + FN should be the number of truly-M items scored, and FP + TN the truly-C items. If they are not 20 and 20, some items were unparseable — go and find out how many and which.

Check the total number of M predictions. That is TP + FP. If the aware condition's recall jumped and TP + FP is close to 40, you have not found a better detector; you have found a model that learned to say "misconception" more often. Phase 1's Exercise 1.3 is that failure in pure form: TP = 20, FN = 0, FP = 20, TN = 0 gives 100% recall and an accuracy sitting exactly on the 50% floor.

Read the false negatives themselves. Open the predictions CSV, filter to rows where ground_truth is M and prediction is C, and read the response_text and model_reason columns. This is TODO 6 item 3 in analyze.py and it is usually the most interesting part of the whole project. Were the misses all on one misconception? All longer? All the ones where the wrong belief is implied rather than stated? A sentence like "seven of the nine misses were responses where the misconception was implied by a confident tone rather than stated as a claim" is worth more to a reader than any of your four metrics, because it is a finding nobody could have guessed.

3 · The interval, and why it dominates everything at n = 40

A proportion computed from forty items is not a fact, it is an estimate, and an estimate with a range attached. The range at this sample size is enormous, and it is the single most important thing on this page.

Suppose your aware condition got 30 of the 40 items right — 75.0% accuracy, comfortably above the 50% majority-class floor. The 95% Wilson interval for 30/40 is 59.8% to 85.8%. That is the honest statement of what you know: the true accuracy of this prompt on this kind of item is somewhere in a band twenty-six percentage points wide. You do not know it to the nearest five points. You do not know it to the nearest ten.

The Wilson score interval cited

For \(k\) successes out of \(n\), with \(\hat p = k/n\) and \(z = 1.96\) for 95%:

$$p_{\pm} \;=\; \frac{\hat p + \dfrac{z^{2}}{2n} \;\pm\; z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p\,(1-\hat p)}{n} + \dfrac{z^{2}}{4n^{2}}}}{1 + \dfrac{z^{2}}{n}}$$

In the site's engine this is G.wilson(k, n), returning [lo, hi]. Use it everywhere. The textbook interval \(\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\) is not merely less accurate here — at 38/40 it produces an upper bound of 1.018, an accuracy of 101.8%, which is visibly not a thing that exists. Wilson gives 83.5% to 98.6% and never leaves the unit interval by construction.

Now put two of these side by side, because that is what you are actually going to do. Suppose baseline got 28/40 and aware got 30/40 — a five-point difference in accuracy, the kind of gap that looks decisive on a bar chart.

Five points, at n = 40

baseline 28/40 = 70.0%, 95% Wilson interval 54.6% to 81.9% (width 27.4 points)

aware 30/40 = 75.0%, 95% Wilson interval 59.8% to 85.8% (width 26.0 points)

The two intervals share the range 59.8% to 81.9%, which is 22.1 points wide — that is 85% of the aware interval and 81% of the baseline interval. They are very nearly the same interval. A five-point difference at n = 40 is two items. Two students writing slightly differently would erase it.

This is not a defect in your study. It is what forty items buys, and it was known before you started; a pilot is for establishing that a method runs end to end and for measuring roughly how big an effect might be, not for settling anything. What it does mean is that the honest conclusion from most plausible outcomes of this pilot is "consistent with an effect of the size we hoped for, and also consistent with no effect" — and saying that clearly, in one sentence, is a better piece of work than claiming a significance you do not have. A reader who catches you overclaiming stops believing the rest of the page. A reader who watches you state your own limits believes everything else you say.

Lab 1 · The interval overlap lab

Recall is computed on the twenty truly-M items only, so both sliders are counts out of 20. Set the two conditions and read the two Wilson intervals as horizontal bars, with their shared range shaded. Try the difference you are hoping for. Then try to find any pair where the intervals stop overlapping — you will need a gap of about eight or nine items out of twenty, which is a forty-point difference. The readout says what you are entitled to conclude, and roughly how many items it would take to be entitled to more.

Two settings worth trying in that lab. Set both to 14 and 15 — a five-point difference in recall, one item — and read the required-n line: about 1250 truly-M items per condition. Then set them to 11 and 17, the thirty-point difference from Phase 1's worked example, and it drops to about 33. The required sample size scales with the inverse square of the difference you are chasing, which is why small effects are so expensive and why a pilot can only ever detect a large one.

4 · The comparison that matters

Everything above puts an interval around one number. But your study is not about one number. It is about a difference, and the quantity your whole design exists to estimate is

$$\Delta \;=\; \text{recall}_{\text{aware}} - \text{recall}_{\text{baseline}}$$

with recall as always taking M — "this response contains a misconception" — as the positive class. Reporting two intervals and eyeballing whether they overlap is not the same as putting an interval on \(\Delta\), and the difference between those two things is worth understanding, because it is where your design earns its keep.

Here is the key structural fact. The same forty responses go through both conditions. Item S07 is not a fresh draw in the aware condition; it is the identical sentence, judged again by the identical model with a different prompt. So the two sets of predictions are paired, and pairing is powerful: whatever makes S07 intrinsically hard is common to both conditions and cancels out of the difference.

Restrict attention to the twenty truly-M items, since that is what recall is computed on, and define two counts:

  • b — items the baseline missed and the aware condition caught. The flips in your favour.
  • c — items the baseline caught and the aware condition missed. The flips against you.

Then the algebra is immediate, and you can do it on paper:

$$\Delta \;=\; \frac{TP_{\text{aware}} - TP_{\text{baseline}}}{20} \;=\; \frac{b - c}{20}$$

Items both conditions caught, and items both conditions missed, contribute nothing to the difference. Only the flips do. This is why counting b and c by hand is already most of the value of the analysis: it is two numbers, it takes ten minutes with the two predictions CSVs open side by side, and it tells you something the marginals cannot.

Why the marginals cannot tell you proved

Baseline recall 11/20, aware recall 17/20. The marginals fix only \(b - c = 6\). They are consistent with any of:

b = 6, c = 0 — six clean gains, nothing lost.
b = 8, c = 2 — eight gained, two given back.
b = 10, c = 4 — ten gained, four given back.

All three give exactly \(\Delta = 30\) percentage points, and all three give exactly the same pair of Wilson intervals, because Wilson only sees 11/20 and 17/20. But they are very different results. The first says the catalogue adds detections without disturbing anything. The third says the catalogue is churning the model's verdicts and happens to come out ahead — fourteen of twenty items changed answer. You cannot tell them apart from metrics.csv. You can tell them apart in ten minutes with the predictions files. ∎

An interval on \(\Delta\) that uses the pairing exists and is worth quoting. With \(n\) the number of truly-M items and \(z = 1.96\) cited:

$$\Delta \;\pm\; z\,\frac{\sqrt{\,(b+c) - \dfrac{(b-c)^{2}}{n}\,}}{n}$$

Running the three cases above, all with \(\Delta = 0.30\) and \(n = 20\):

The same difference, three different intervals

b = 6, c = 0. \(b+c = 6\), \((b-c)^2/n = 36/20 = 1.8\), so the square root is \(\sqrt{4.2} = 2.049\). Standard error \(2.049/20 = 0.1025\); half-width \(1.96 \times 0.1025 = 0.201\). Interval: +9.9 to +50.1 points.

b = 8, c = 2. \(\sqrt{10 - 1.8} = \sqrt{8.2} = 2.864\); half-width \(1.96 \times 2.864/20 = 0.281\). Interval: +1.9 to +58.1 points.

b = 10, c = 4. \(\sqrt{14 - 1.8} = \sqrt{12.2} = 3.493\); half-width \(1.96 \times 3.493/20 = 0.342\). Interval: −4.2 to +64.2 points.

Identical marginals, identical point estimate, and the third interval contains zero while the first does not. The pairing is doing all the work.

McNemar's test

The formal test corresponding to that argument has a name. McNemar's test is the correct significance test for two sets of binary predictions made on the same items cited. It looks only at the discordant pairs — the flips — and asks whether they are lopsided enough to be surprising if the two conditions were equivalent. Under the null hypothesis that the conditions do not differ, each flip is equally likely to go either way, so \(b\) is a fair coin tossed \(b+c\) times. The continuity-corrected statistic is

$$\chi^{2} \;=\; \frac{\bigl(|b - c| - 1\bigr)^{2}}{b + c}$$

compared against a chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom, whose 5% critical value is 3.841 cited. Larger means less consistent with "no difference".

When McNemar is the right test, and when it is not

Use it when the same items were classified twice — which is your entire design. It is the right test for baseline versus aware on your forty items, and for baseline versus aware on the twenty truly-M items separately.

Do not use it to compare your study with somebody else's, or two different sets of items. Different items are not paired and the whole argument collapses.

When \(b+c\) is small — and at n = 40 it will be — the exact binomial test is preferred cited: compute the two-sided probability of seeing a split at least this lopsided from \(b+c\) fair coin tosses. The chi-square version is an approximation to it, and the approximation is poor with a handful of flips. With \(b = 6, c = 0\) the exact two-sided probability is \(2 \times (1/2)^{6} = 0.031\), which you can compute on paper; the chi-square version gives \(25/6 = 4.167\) and a probability of about 0.041. Lab 2 prints both.

And the thing to hold on to: reporting the paired counts is already most of the value. "Of the forty items, six changed from wrong under the baseline to right under the aware prompt, and none changed the other way" is a sentence any reader can evaluate, needs no test, and cannot be wrong. The test adds a number on top of it. If you never get to the test, report the counts and you have still done the analysis honestly.

Lab 2 · The paired-flip lab

All forty items now, scored right or wrong under each condition. Set how many flipped from wrong-under-baseline to right-under-aware (b) and how many flipped the other way (c). The two shaded cells are the only ones McNemar looks at; the greyed diagonal is everything both conditions agreed on, and it is discarded. 5 · Reporting the decoy

Section 4 put an interval on the gap between baseline and aware. This section is about what that gap means, which is a separate question and the one a reader will actually press you on.

Recall the problem from Phase 1. The misconception-aware prompt changes two things at once: the model is handed a catalogue, and the prompt becomes longer, more structured and more specific. A gap in recall is consistent with either. The decoy condition holds the second fixed and removes the first — same framing sentence, same numbered catalogue of ten documented misconceptions, same instruction, comparable length, but about physics, which cannot help with a password answer. It was declared in plans/RESEARCH_PLAN.md §3.1 before any data existed.

Where each part of it goes in the write-up

Methods — one sentence, and it must contain the word pre-registered or something that means it, because the whole value of a placebo declared in advance is that it was declared in advance.

Results — as a diagnostic alongside the main comparison, with its own confusion matrix and its own row in Table 1, immediately after the baseline-versus-aware paragraph so the reader meets it while the gap is still in mind. Not in its own section, not at the end, and not framed as a third arm.

Limitations — either the topical-relevance caveat if you ran it, or the sentence admitting you did not. One or the other appears; neither is optional.

Nowhere — in a set of pairwise significance tests against everything else. The pre-registered comparison is baseline against misconception_aware, full stop. The decoy interprets that one gap. Three arms tested against each other is three chances to be fooled where the design budgeted for one, and at n = 40 you cannot afford the extra two. Say this explicitly in the write-up and the multiple-comparisons objection never gets raised.

Read it exactly the way you read the other two matrices: the false-negative cell first. Here is the running example of this page — baseline 11/20 and aware 17/20 from section 2 — with a decoy run of 12/20 beside them:

Twelve of the twenty misconceptions caught by a prompt carrying a catalogue of physics misconceptions, against eleven for the plain prompt and seventeen for the real one. A single item's difference is well inside the noise at n = 20 positives, so the honest reading is that the decoy moved nothing this design can detect — which is what leaves the aware condition's gap standing. Check the alarm counts too, which is the same trick as section 2: TP + FP is 15 for the baseline, 18 for the decoy and 24 for the aware condition. The catalogue shape alone made this model somewhat more willing to answer M without making it much better at it, and that is a small honest observation you get for free once the condition exists.

Now the interpretation, written down in Phase 1 before any of these numbers existed, which is the only reason you are allowed to apply it now:

ResultWhat it means
aware > decoy ≈ baseline The catalogue helped because of its content. The hoped-for result, and the one the example above shows.
aware ≈ decoy > baseline Any structured catalogue helped. A real finding, just a different one — and the one that would otherwise have been published by mistake, as a claim about misconception knowledge that was really a claim about prompt shape.
decoy < baseline The irrelevant catalogue actively confused the model, which is evidence it genuinely reads the list rather than ignoring it.

A worked sentence for Results, in the register of the rest of the page — numbers with denominators, no adjectives, the interval attached:

"The pre-registered decoy condition — the same catalogue prompt carrying ten documented physics misconceptions in place of the ten password ones — caught 12 of the 20 items containing a misconception (60.0%, 95% CI 38.7–78.1%), one item more than the baseline's 11/20 and five fewer than the misconception-aware condition's 17/20. The gain under the misconception-aware prompt is therefore not accounted for by prompt length or structure alone. The decoy is reported as a diagnostic for interpreting that gap and was not part of the pre-registered comparison."

And the sentence for Limitations if you ran it, which names the thing the decoy does not control for before anybody else does:

"The decoy holds prompt length, structure and framing fixed, but it does not control for topical relevance: a physics catalogue is both irrelevant to the item and outside the domain of the question, so a decoy effect could reflect either. A stricter placebo would list ten true statements about passwords — same domain, same length, same numbered form, no misconceptions in it — and that is the version this study would run next."
If you did not run it, Limitations must say so

Running the decoy is optional; leaving it out of the write-up is not. Forty extra model calls costs about five minutes, and the term may simply run out, which is a fine reason. What is not fine is a Results section that reads as though the question never came up.

"A decoy condition — the same catalogue prompt carrying ten physics misconceptions instead of the ten password ones — was pre-registered but not run, for reasons of time. Without it, a reader cannot tell whether the catalogue's content is what helped or whether any longer, more structured prompt would have done the same, because the misconception-aware prompt varies both together. Running it is the first thing we would do next; it costs one flag and about five minutes."

Three sentences. They cost you nothing, they are the first thing an experienced reader would have asked, and writing them yourself is the difference between a limitation and a hole.

6 · Five ways to fool yourself

None of these requires dishonesty. Every one of them is something a careful, well-meaning person does by accident, which is exactly why they have names and why you check for them deliberately rather than trusting yourself.

1. Reporting accuracy without the 50% floor

Your dataset is twenty M and twenty C by construction, so the always-guess-M classifier scores 50% and contains no information about passwords at all. An accuracy of 60% is therefore ten points above nothing, not sixty points above nothing — and the Wilson interval for 24/40 is 44.6% to 73.7%, which contains 50%. The fix: every accuracy you write down carries the floor in the same sentence. Not "the model got 60%", but "60.0% (95% CI 44.6–73.7%) against a majority-class floor of 50%, which the interval contains".

2. Reporting a difference smaller than the interval

A five-point difference at n = 40 is two items, and section 3 showed that the two intervals around it are almost the same interval. Writing "the aware prompt improved accuracy by 5 points" is not false as arithmetic and is badly misleading as a claim, because it presents as a finding something the data cannot distinguish from two students having written differently. The fix: compute the interval before you write the sentence, not after. If the interval on the difference contains zero, the word "improved" does not appear; the words "consistent with" do.

3. Dropping the unparseable items instead of reporting them

Some replies will not contain a readable verdict. parse_verdict() returns ? rather than guessing, and compute_confusion() counts those into the unparseable column and excludes them from the four cells. That is the correct behaviour and it creates a reporting obligation, because it quietly changes your denominators: if five items are unparseable in one condition and none in the other, you are comparing a proportion out of 35 with a proportion out of 40, and those are not the same measurement.

It is also a real finding in its own right: "the 3B model produced unreadable output on 12% of items under the aware prompt and on 2% under the baseline" tells a reader something true about small models and long prompts. Hiding it makes your numbers look better than they are, which is why the code refuses to default them to C. The fix: report the count for each condition in the same paragraph as the metrics, and if the counts differ much between conditions, report the metrics both ways — excluding those items, and counting them all as errors.

4. Changing the prompt after seeing the results and reporting the best one

This is the serious one, and it feels completely innocent. You run the study; the aware condition is barely ahead; you notice the catalogue would read better with the misconceptions numbered; you rerun; now it is four points ahead; you write that up. Nothing you did was a lie and the result is still worthless, because you made a choice after seeing the data and you made it in the direction you were hoping for.

Researcher degrees of freedom cited

Every study contains dozens of small decisions with no obviously right answer: which prompt wording, whether to exclude an item that reads oddly, whether to count a borderline reply as M. Each one is defensible on its own. Taken together, and chosen after looking at the results, they let a careful person produce a positive finding from data containing no effect — without ever knowingly doing anything wrong. The general name for the practice is p-hacking; the general name for the freedom that enables it is researcher degrees of freedom.

The fix is not "never change the prompt". Iterating on a prompt is a reasonable thing to do and you will learn from it. The fix is to report every prompt you tried and every result it produced, in a table, in the order you tried them, with a sentence saying which one you had committed to in advance. Three prompts with three results, all shown, is honest and interesting. One prompt with one result, chosen from three, is not — and the only difference between them is what you chose to print.

Two practical protections, both cheap. Commit your predicted outcome to the repository with a date before Phase 4 runs, as Phase 1 exercise 1.7 asked. And keep every version of prompts.py in git history rather than editing over it, so the record of what you tried exists whether or not you remember to write it down.

5. Attributing a real effect to the wrong cause

This one is different from the other four, and it is the reason the decoy exists. Suppose everything above goes right: the effect is real, the interval excludes zero, the flips are lopsided your way, no prompt was revised after seeing anything. You write "supplying a catalogue of known misconceptions raised recall by twenty points". Every number in that sentence is correct. The sentence can still be false, because your intervention changed two things at once — the catalogue and the shape of the prompt — and the data cannot say which one moved the model. You have measured a real effect and attributed it to the wrong cause, and no amount of statistical care catches that, because it is not a statistical error. It is a design error, and it can only be fixed before the run.

The fix is a control condition that varies one of the two and holds the other fixed, which is what section 5 is about. Note the shape of the defence: the other four failures are caught by being careful with numbers you already have, and this one cannot be. If the decoy is not in the design, the objection is unanswerable at write-up time — the only honest response is to name it in Limitations and move on, which is exactly why the sentence in section 5 is not optional.

And note what makes it defensible: the decoy was written into the plan before any run. A placebo added after seeing a disappointing gap is failure mode 4 wearing the costume of failure mode 5 — it looks like rigour and it is another decision made in the light of the results. Declaring it in advance costs nothing and settles the question permanently. If you are ever asked what pre-registration is actually for, this is the example: not a form to fill in, but a control condition you would never have been able to add later.

7 · The write-up skeleton

Three sections. Write Methods before you have results — it keeps you honest, and it is the part you can do while the run is still going.

Methods — five paragraphs

1. The items. How many, how built, how balanced, from what source list. "I wrote 40 short responses covering 10 misconceptions about passwords and authentication, drawn from a longer catalogue; each misconception has two responses containing it and two correct responses on the same topic, giving 20 M and 20 C."

2. The labelling. Who labelled, whether anyone checked, and how much they agreed. "All 40 items were written and labelled by the author; a second reader independently labelled 12 of them and agreed on 11." If there was no second reader, say that in the same plain way.

3. The model. Name, version, how it was run, and every setting that could change the answer. "Responses were classified by llama3.2:3b run locally through Ollama at temperature 0, with a 200-token reply cap, on a single laptop." If you catch yourself writing "I used an AI model", go back and add which one.

4. The two conditions. Both prompts, described, with the difference named exactly — and the place where a second thing had to differ named too. "The two prompts are identical except that the misconception-aware prompt lists the ten catalogue statements and instructs the model to check the response against them; the instruction sentence therefore differs as well, which is a second difference and is discussed in the limitations."

4b. The decoy. One sentence, in Methods rather than in Results, and it must say when it was declared. "A third prompt, pre-registered before any data was collected, is identical to the misconception-aware prompt in framing, structure, instruction and approximate length but lists ten documented misconceptions about physics in place of the ten about passwords; it is reported as a diagnostic for interpreting the difference between the two pre-registered conditions, not as a third experimental arm." If you did not run it, this paragraph still describes it and Limitations says it was not executed.

5. The measurement. The confusion matrix, the four metrics, the positive class, and why recall leads. "Predictions were scored into a 2×2 confusion matrix with 'the response contains a misconception' as the positive class; accuracy, precision, recall and F1 were computed from it, and recall is reported as the primary outcome because a missed misconception is the error that carries a cost to the student."

Results — five paragraphs and one table

1. The table. Table 1 is metrics.csv with two columns added by hand: the 95% Wilson interval for recall and for accuracy. Caption it so it stands alone: "Table 1. Confusion-matrix counts and detection metrics for the two prompts (llama3.2:3b, temperature 0, 40 responses, positive class = contains a misconception)."

2. Plain reporting, no adjectives. Numbers with their denominators, neutral verbs, no "impressively", no "only", no interpretation yet. "Recall was 17/20 (85.0%, 95% CI 64.0–94.8%) with the misconception catalogue and 11/20 (55.0%, 34.2–74.2%) without it; precision fell from 11/15 (73.3%) to 17/24 (70.8%). All 80 judgements parsed as a clear M or C, so no items were excluded."

3. The paired comparison. The flips, then the test. "Of the 20 items containing a misconception, 8 were missed by the baseline and caught by the aware condition and 2 went the other way (McNemar chi-square = 2.50, exact two-sided p = 0.109), giving a difference in recall of +30.0 points (95% CI +1.9 to +58.1)."

4. The decoy, if you ran it. Straight after the paired comparison, while the gap is still in the reader's mind, and labelled as a diagnostic. "The pre-registered decoy condition caught 12/20 (60.0%, 95% CI 38.7–78.1%), one item above the baseline and five below the misconception-aware condition, so the observed gain is not accounted for by prompt length or structure alone; the decoy was not part of the pre-registered comparison and no significance test was run against it." If you did not run it, this paragraph does not exist and the sentence in Limitations does instead.

5. What the errors looked like. The false negatives, read and characterised, with one or two quoted. This is the paragraph a reader remembers.

Limitations — a list, in your own voice

Naming the holes yourself is what makes the rest believable; someone else will find them anyway. Start from this list, cut what does not apply, and add what you found.

  • n = 40. Two or three items going the other way moves any of these numbers by five to seven points, and the 95% intervals are twenty to forty points wide. This is a pilot, not a measurement.
  • One domain. Passwords and authentication only. Nothing here says anything about phishing, encryption, or any subject outside computer security.
  • One model. A single 3-billion-parameter model at one temperature. A larger model, or a differently trained one of the same size, might behave completely differently.
  • Single-author labelling. The same person wrote the items and assigned the labels, so any systematic idea of what counts as a misconception is baked into both.
  • Hand-authored responses. The forty items were written to contain misconceptions, not collected from students. Real student writing is messier, shorter, and often ambiguous in ways these items are not.
  • Detection, not learning. This measures whether misconceptions get noticed. It says nothing about whether anyone learns anything, which is the question the project is ultimately about.
  • The decoy, if it was not run. "A decoy condition was pre-registered but not run, for reasons of time. Without it, a reader cannot tell whether the catalogue's content is what helped or whether any longer, more structured prompt would have done the same." Delete this bullet only if you ran it.
  • What the decoy does not control, if it was run. Prompt length, structure and framing are held fixed; topical relevance is not. A physics catalogue is both irrelevant and off-domain, so those two explanations remain tangled. A stricter placebo would list ten true statements about passwords.

8 · What would make this a stronger study

Ranked by cost, cheapest first. The ordering matters: the first two are afternoons, the last one is a term.

1. A second model. Change MODEL_NAME in config.py and rerun: one more afternoon, no new data, and you have doubled the evidence about generality. If the catalogue helps both llama3.2:3b and llama3.2:1b that is much harder to dismiss than either alone; if it helps one and not the other, that is a result about model size nobody could have predicted. Best value per hour available to you.

2. A second labeller. Hand the forty responses, stripped of labels, to someone competent and ask them to label them cold; report the agreement. One person, one hour, and it attacks the biggest weakness in the design — that whoever decided what counts as a misconception also wrote the items. High agreement makes your ground truth defensible; low agreement is something important you learned before anyone else pointed it out.

3. Naturally occurring student responses. Real answers rather than answers written to order. This is the single largest improvement in what the study means, since it removes the possibility that you unconsciously wrote items your model could handle. Third rather than first only because it needs permission and permission needs time. Even twenty real responses alongside your forty would show whether the metrics hold on writing you did not author.

4. More items. The obvious fix, deliberately last, because the required-n line in Lab 1 shows why: detecting a five-point difference needs over a thousand items per condition, which is not a school project. Interval width shrinks roughly with the square root of n, so doubling from 40 to 80 narrows it by only about 30%. More items is the right move once you already know the effect is large enough to chase, and the pilot is what tells you that.

9 · Exercises

Exercise 5.1 ★★arithmetic

Compute the 95% Wilson interval for a recall of 15/20 by hand, using \(z = 1.96\) so that \(z^2 = 3.8416\). Show every step. Then check your answer against G.wilson(15, 20) by setting the aware slider in Lab 1 to 15.

Solution

\(\hat p = 15/20 = 0.75\), \(n = 20\), \(z^2 = 3.8416\).

Denominator. \(1 + z^2/n = 1 + 3.8416/20 = 1 + 0.19208 = \mathbf{1.19208}\).

Centre. \(\hat p + z^2/(2n) = 0.75 + 3.8416/40 = 0.75 + 0.09604 = \mathbf{0.84604}\). Note it is pulled above 0.75 — the Wilson interval shrinks estimates towards one half, which is why it never runs off the end.

Spread. Inside the root: \(\hat p(1-\hat p)/n = 0.75 \times 0.25/20 = 0.1875/20 = 0.009375\), and \(z^2/(4n^2) = 3.8416/1600 = 0.0024010\). Sum \(= 0.0117760\), root \(= 0.1085173\), times \(z\): \(1.96 \times 0.1085173 = \mathbf{0.212694}\).

Combine.
lower \(= (0.84604 - 0.212694)/1.19208 = 0.633346/1.19208 = \mathbf{0.5313}\)
upper \(= (0.84604 + 0.212694)/1.19208 = 1.058734/1.19208 = \mathbf{0.8881}\)

So 15/20 = 75.0%, 95% CI 53.1% to 88.8%. Lab 1 agrees.

Look at the width: 35.7 percentage points. That is what "the model caught three quarters of them" is worth on twenty items — the truth could be a little over half, or nearly nine in ten. Any sentence you write about this number has to survive both ends of that range.

Exercise 5.2 ★★thinking

Your run gives baseline recall 13/20 and aware recall 15/20. Someone suggests the headline "the misconception catalogue improved recall by 10 percentage points". Decide whether that difference is reportable as an improvement, and say exactly what you would write instead. The Wilson interval for 13/20 is 43.3% to 81.9%; for 15/20 it is 53.1% to 88.8%.

Solution

No, not as an improvement. Three reasons, in the order a reader would raise them.

1. The difference is two items. 15 − 13 = 2 out of twenty. Ten percentage points sounds substantial and is, at this scale, two students' worth of writing.

2. The intervals nearly coincide. 43.3–81.9% and 53.1–88.8% share 53.1–81.9%, a range 28.8 points wide, which is 75% of the first interval and 81% of the second. Both numbers describe close to the same state of knowledge.

3. The paired test cannot rescue it either. The marginals fix \(b - c = 2\). The most favourable possible case is \(b = 2, c = 0\): McNemar's continuity-corrected statistic is \((|2-0|-1)^2/(2+0) = 1/2 = 0.5\), far below 3.841, and the exact two-sided probability from two fair coin tosses is \(2 \times (1/2)^2 = 0.5\). Even in the best case this is a coin landing the same way twice.

What to write: "Recall was 15/20 (75.0%, 95% CI 53.1–88.8%) with the catalogue and 13/20 (65.0%, 43.3–81.9%) without. Two additional items were caught; b = 2 items improved and c = 0 worsened (exact two-sided p = 0.50). This design cannot distinguish a difference of this size from noise, and the result is consistent both with a useful effect and with none."

Then, in Limitations, the required-n figure: detecting a difference between 65% and 75% reliably would need roughly 326 truly-M items per condition, against the 20 you have. Saying that turns "we found nothing" into "here is what finding something would have cost", which is a much more useful sentence for whoever runs this next.

Exercise 5.3 ★★arithmetic

Over all forty items, nine flipped from wrong under the baseline to right under the aware prompt, and three flipped the other way. Compute the net change in accuracy, the continuity-corrected McNemar statistic, and say what you would report. Then state how many items the two conditions agreed on, and why that number does not appear anywhere in the calculation.

Solution

\(b = 9\), \(c = 3\), so \(b + c = 12\) discordant items and \(40 - 12 = \mathbf{28}\) items on which the two conditions agreed.

Net change. \(b - c = 6\) items, and \(6/40 = 0.15\), so accuracy rises by 15.0 percentage points. Every point of that comes from the twelve flips; the twenty-eight agreements contribute exactly nothing, because they contribute the same amount to both conditions and cancel in the subtraction.

McNemar. \(\chi^2 = (|9-3| - 1)^2/(9+3) = (6-1)^2/12 = 25/12 = \mathbf{2.083}\). The 5% critical value for one degree of freedom is 3.841, and 2.083 is well below it.

The exact test, which is the one to quote at \(b+c = 12\). Under the null each flip is a fair coin, so the two-sided probability of a split at least as lopsided as 9–3 is \(2 \sum_{i=0}^{3}\binom{12}{i} / 2^{12} = 2(1 + 12 + 66 + 220)/4096 = 598/4096 = \mathbf{0.146}\).

What to report: "Nine items were wrong under the baseline and right with the catalogue; three went the other way (McNemar chi-square = 2.08, exact two-sided p = 0.146). Accuracy rose by 6 items (15.0 points), a difference this design cannot distinguish from chance."

The twenty-eight agreements are absent from the arithmetic because McNemar asks a question they cannot answer: "when the two conditions disagreed, did they disagree lopsidedly?" An item both prompts got right tells you the item was easy, not which prompt is better. This is also why the test is more sensitive than comparing the two accuracies: it throws away the shared difficulty instead of letting it dilute the signal.

Exercise 5.4 ★★writing

Here is a sentence from a first draft. Identify every separate thing wrong with it, then write a replacement that says everything true it was trying to say.

"Our results prove that giving an AI a list of misconceptions makes it significantly better at detecting student misconceptions, with accuracy improving from 70% to 76%."

The underlying run: baseline TP = 14, FN = 6, FP = 6, TN = 14, none unparseable; aware TP = 17, FN = 3, FP = 6, TN = 12, with two further items unparseable and therefore excluded, both of them items you had labelled C. Over the 38 items scored under both conditions the paired flips were b = 7, c = 5.

Solution

Six separate problems.

1. "prove". Forty items never prove anything. The correct verbs are "suggests", "is consistent with", "is early evidence for".

2. "an AI". Unreproducible. It was llama3.2:3b at temperature 0, run locally through Ollama, and a reader cannot repeat the study without that.

3. "significantly". This word has a technical meaning and here it is simply false. The paired flips were b = 7 against c = 5, so the McNemar statistic is \((|7-5|-1)^2/12 = 1/12 = 0.083\) and the exact two-sided probability from twelve coin tosses is 0.774. Nothing about this is significant in any sense.

4. No interval, and no floor. Baseline accuracy is 28/40 = 70.0% with a Wilson interval of 54.6–81.9%; aware accuracy is 29/38 = 76.3% with an interval of 60.8–87.0%. They overlap over 21.1 points, and both sit above a majority-class floor of 50% that has to be stated for the numbers to mean anything.

5. Accuracy is the wrong headline. The interesting change here is recall, 14/20 to 17/20, which accuracy cannot see: it cannot distinguish six misses from six false alarms, and this project exists because those are not the same error.

6. The unparseable items are invisible. Two of the aware condition's items were dropped, so its counts total 38, not 40 — its accuracy and precision have a different denominator from the baseline's and the reader is not told.

A replacement:

"With the misconception catalogue in the prompt, llama3.2:3b (temperature 0, run locally) caught 17 of the 20 responses containing a misconception (85.0%, 95% CI 64.0–94.8%), against 14 of 20 without it (70.0%, 48.1–85.5%). Two further items in the aware condition produced unparseable output and are excluded, so its accuracy is computed on 38 items rather than 40; both were items labelled correct, so recall is unaffected. Across the 38 items scored under both conditions, 7 changed from wrong to right and 5 from right to wrong (McNemar chi-square = 0.08, exact two-sided p = 0.77). Accuracy was 29/38 = 76.3% (60.8–87.0%) with the catalogue and 28/40 = 70.0% (54.6–81.9%) without, against a majority-class floor of 50%. These data are consistent with a recall improvement of the size we predicted and also with no effect; at n = 40 the design cannot separate the two."

It is four times as long and it is the only version a reader will believe. Notice also what the flip counts revealed that the marginals hid: twelve of the thirty-eight verdicts changed, to produce a net gain of two items. The catalogue is churning this model's judgements rather than sharpening them, and that sentence is a genuine finding you would have missed entirely by reading metrics.csv alone.

Exercise 5.5 ★★by hand

The aware condition returns five unparseable replies, three of them on items you labelled M. So its recall is computed on 17 truly-M items, not 20, and it caught 14 of them. The baseline had none unparseable and caught 11 of 20. Work out the range that the aware condition's recall could take once you account for the dropped items, and write the sentence you would put in Results.

Solution

As computed by the script: 14/17 = 82.4%, 95% Wilson interval 59.0% to 93.8%. This is what metrics.csv will say, and quoting it without the caveat compares a proportion out of 17 with the baseline's proportion out of 20.

The bounds. The three dropped items really were misconceptions; the model simply failed to say anything readable about them. Two extreme accountings:

Best case — treat all three as catches: 17/20 = 85.0% (64.0–94.8%).
Worst case — treat all three as misses, which is what a deployed tutor would effectively do, since an unreadable verdict corrects nobody: 14/20 = 70.0% (48.1–85.5%).

So the aware condition's recall is somewhere in 70.0% to 85.0% depending purely on a bookkeeping decision, against the baseline's 11/20 = 55.0% (34.2–74.2%). The worst-case figure still exceeds the baseline point estimate, which is worth saying — the conclusion does not flip under the least generous accounting.

What to write: "Five of the aware condition's 40 replies could not be parsed as a clear verdict (12.5%), three of them on items containing a misconception; the baseline produced none. Recall computed on the 17 scored M items was 14/17 (82.4%, 95% CI 59.0–93.8%). Counting all three unparseable M items as missed — the conservative reading, since an unreadable verdict corrects nobody — gives 14/20 (70.0%, 48.1–85.5%), against 11/20 (55.0%, 34.2–74.2%) for the baseline."

Two things follow. Reporting the metric both ways costs one extra sentence and removes any suspicion that you chose the flattering denominator. And the asymmetry itself is a finding: the longer aware prompt produced unreadable output where the short one did not, which is a real and reportable property of a 3B model given a long prompt, and belongs in the Results rather than being treated as an embarrassment.

Exercise 5.6 ★★writing

Write the paragraph headed "What would have changed our conclusion" for your actual results, once you have them. It must name at least two specific numerical outcomes that you would have accepted as evidence against your own hypothesis, and it must be checkable by a stranger holding only your results table. Under 130 words.

Solution

There is no single right answer, but a good one is specific enough that somebody could hold it against your table and tick it off. For example:

"We predicted in advance that the catalogue would raise recall substantially while leaving precision flat or slightly lower. We would have concluded it did not help if recall had risen by two items or fewer out of twenty, since a two-item change gives an exact two-sided p of 0.50 even in the most favourable paired arrangement; or if the paired flips had been near-balanced, say b and c within two of each other, indicating churn rather than improvement; or if the aware condition's total M predictions had exceeded about 32 of 40, indicating drift towards the always-guess-M classifier rather than better detection. We would also have reported a fall in recall, which is a plausible outcome for a 3B model given a long prompt."

Check yours against three questions. Does it name numbers rather than adjectives? Does at least one of the outcomes describe a result that would have embarrassed you? Could a stranger verify, from your results table alone, whether the condition was met? If yes to all three it does its job.

And the timestamp is the whole point. Written before Phase 4 it is a prediction; written afterwards it is a description of what happened, dressed up as one.

Exercise 5.7 ★★★research

You run the study. The aware condition's recall is 12/20 against the baseline's 11/20 — one item. You reread the aware prompt, decide the catalogue would be clearer numbered and with a blank line between entries, rerun, and get 15/20. You now have two results and a choice. Say exactly what you would do, what you would report, and what you would say at the check-in when asked why there are two runs.

Solution

What not to do: report 15/20 and delete the first run. Nothing about that is a lie — the second run really did give 15/20 — and it is still worthless, because the prompt was chosen after seeing that the first one underperformed, and chosen in the direction you wanted. That is researcher degrees of freedom in its purest form, and the reason it is dangerous is exactly that it does not feel like cheating.

What to do. Report both, in a table, in the order you ran them, with the committed prompt marked:

Prompt A (pre-registered, plain list): recall 12/20 = 60.0%, 95% CI 38.7–78.1%
Prompt B (revised after seeing A, numbered list): recall 15/20 = 75.0%, 95% CI 53.1–88.8%
Baseline: recall 11/20 = 55.0%, 95% CI 34.2–74.2%

Then say plainly which comparison is your result. Prompt A against baseline is the study — one item's difference, which is nothing. Prompt B against baseline is a hypothesis generated by this pilot and not tested by it, and the honest description of it is "a promising second prompt that would need a fresh set of items to evaluate".

At the check-in. Say it before you are asked: "I ran the pre-registered prompt first and got almost nothing. I then revised the prompt after seeing that, and the revision did better, so I am reporting both and treating the revision as something to test next rather than as my result, because I chose it after looking at the data." That answer is much stronger than a clean single number, because it demonstrates you know what the trap is. Anyone experienced will immediately ask whether you tried more than one prompt; having the answer ready and honest is worth more than the extra fifteen points would have been.

The cheap fix for next time, worth writing into the plan: hold back ten items from the start, never look at them, and use them once at the end to evaluate whatever prompt you ended up with. That gives you a legitimate test of the revised prompt without needing to collect anything new.

Going deeper (optional)

Why the interval narrows so slowly. The half-width of an interval on a proportion is roughly proportional to \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so to halve it you need four times as many items and to shrink it tenfold you need a hundred times as many. That single fact governs the economics of every empirical study ever run, and it is why "collect more data" is almost never the cheapest improvement available.

Newcombe's methods. The paired interval used in section 4 is a Wald-type approximation. Better intervals for the difference of two proportions — including the paired case — are given by Newcombe's hybrid score methods, which combine Wilson intervals for the individual proportions rather than assuming normality of the difference cited. Worth naming if anyone asks what a statistician would actually use; not worth implementing for a pilot, where the honest conclusion does not turn on the third decimal place.
