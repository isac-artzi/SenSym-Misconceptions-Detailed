Wrong in a Particular Way · Phase 1
Phase 1 · P1Win: you can defend the design without notes

The design, and why it is this shape

Two conditions, one difference, forty items, four numbers, and a prediction written down in advance.

This is the page you will be asked about — not the code, not the charts, the reasoning. Somebody will say "why only detection?", "why is recall the headline?", "why not just report accuracy?", and the answer has to come out of your mouth in a sentence rather than off a slide. Everything here is arithmetic you can do on paper or a choice you can justify in one line. No code this phase, nothing to install.

By the end of this phase you can

  • Say why the pilot measures detection rather than learning, and why that is a narrowing rather than a retreat.
  • State the two conditions and name, exactly, the one thing that differs between them.
  • Fill in a confusion matrix from a list of labels and predictions, by hand, with M as the positive class.
  • Compute accuracy, precision, recall and F1 from that matrix, and say which one you lead with and why.
  • Explain why F1 is a harmonic mean, using the arithmetic rather than the word "balance".
  • State the majority-class floor for this design and refuse to report any number without it.
  • Say in advance what result would count as "the catalogue did not help".

1 · The question, and why the pilot measures detection

The question the project is really about is this: does a language model that knows the catalogue of misconceptions help someone learn more than one that does not? This pilot does not answer it and cannot. Answering it needs real students, two groups, a pre-test, a post-test, a delayed post-test weeks later, parental consent and a school sign-off — a year of work, most of it paperwork.

So the pilot takes the question apart. "Misconception-aware tutoring helps learning" is a chain, and each link depends on the one below it:

The chain, from the top down

How much does the tutoring help someone learn? — depends on →

How often does the tutor say the right thing back? — depends on →

How reliably does the tutor notice the misconception at all? this link, and only this link

Read the chain from the bottom. A tutor that cannot tell a confused answer from a correct one cannot possibly correct it: it would be congratulating the students who are wrong and correcting the students who are right, and no amount of eloquence downstream rescues that. Detection is a precondition for correction. If detection is unreliable, the higher links have nothing to stand on — and you have found that out in a term rather than in a year.

That is what narrowing looks like when it is done properly. You are not giving up on the big question; you are choosing the one link in the chain that can actually be measured with a laptop and five hours a week. The narrowed question is:

The pilot question

To what extent can a small local language model sort a short written answer about passwords into "contains a misconception" or "correct" — and how much does handing it an explicit catalogue of ten known misconceptions change that?

Note the shape: to what extent, not can it. A yes-or-no question either has an obvious answer or is hiding a measurement; this one forces a number, and a number forces a method.

The honest cost, which goes in your limitations paragraph and not in a footnote: this pilot shows nothing at all about learning. It shows something about detection. Saying so plainly is what makes the rest of the write-up believable.

2 · Two conditions, one difference

Two conditions, named in python/misconception/config.py as CONDITIONS = ["baseline", "misconception_aware"].

baseline is a generic grader: handed one student response, asked whether it is correct or contains an incorrect belief, never told what a password misconception looks like. It stands in for an ordinary chatbot. misconception_aware is the same request with your catalogue of ten misconceptions pasted in above it and an instruction to check against that list.

Everything else is identical: same model (llama3.2:3b), same temperature (0), same forty responses in the same order, same reply-length cap, same parser. Side by side — grey is character-for-character the same in both, highlighted is what differs.

You are evaluating a student's answer
about computer security.

Read the student's response below and
decide whether it is correct or
whether it contains an incorrect
belief.

STUDENT RESPONSE:
"I change my password every month so
my account is definitely secure."

Answer using exactly this format and
nothing else:

VERDICT: <M or C>
REASON: <one short sentence>

Use M if the response contains a
misconception (a confident but
incorrect belief). Use C if the
response is correct.
B · misconception_aware

about computer security.

The following are documented
misconceptions that students commonly
hold about passwords and

1. Long passwords are always strong.
2. Changing passwords often makes
   accounts safer.
   ... all ten, every time ...

Read the student's response below.
Decide whether it reflects any of the
misconceptions listed above, or
whether it is correct.

STUDENT RESPONSE:
"I change my password every month so
my account is definitely secure."

Answer using exactly this format and
nothing else:

VERDICT: <M or C>
REASON: <one short sentence>

Use M if the response contains a
misconception (a confident but
incorrect belief). Use C if the
response is correct.

The principle underneath this is the whole of experimental design in one sentence: a comparison means something only when exactly one thing differs. Suppose you had also made prompt B longer and better organised. Then B wins and you have learned nothing, because you cannot say whether it won on the catalogue or on the tidier writing. There is a word for that — confounded — and a confounded result is not a weak result, it is a result that does not exist.

Where this design honestly stretches its own rule

Look at the highlighted middle of prompt B. The instruction sentence is not identical to A's: it had to change, because B has to point at the list. That is a second difference, and you should say so out loud before somebody says it to you.

The defence is that the change is forced — there is no way to supply a catalogue without referring to it — and that it is as small as it could be. The stronger defence is a control condition that separates the two, and Exercise 1.5 asks you to design it. Knowing where your own design is weakest, and having already thought about the fix, is worth more at a check-in than pretending it is airtight.

Three of these fairness conditions are checked automatically. In python/tests/test_pipeline.py:

  • test_baseline_prompt_does_not_leak_the_misconception_list — no misconception statement appears anywhere in the baseline prompt. If one did, the plain condition would secretly not be plain.
  • test_aware_prompt_contains_every_misconception — every statement in the catalogue actually reaches the model. A quietly truncated list would mean condition B is not the condition you described.
  • test_both_prompts_share_the_same_output_format — OUTPUT_FORMAT_INSTRUCTION appears verbatim in both, so the parser does the same job either way.

Those are necessary conditions, not full identity: they do not check that the two prompts match byte for byte outside the catalogue block, because they do not. Reading them side by side and listing every difference stays your job.

3 · The confusion matrix

After a run you have forty rows, each holding your label and the model's verdict. Every row falls into exactly one of four boxes. Which four depends on a choice you make first, and it is the choice that everything else is measured against:

The positive class is M

M — "this response contains a misconception" — is the positive class. This is set in python/misconception/config.py as POSITIVE_LABEL = LABEL_MISCONCEPTION. Precision, recall and F1 are all measured with respect to it, and every one of them changes if you flip the choice.

"Positive" does not mean good. It means the thing the detector is trying to find. A smoke alarm's positive class is smoke. A metric quoted without its positive class stated is meaningless, so say it every single time you print one.

With M as positive, the four boxes are:

  • TP — truly M, model said M: it caught a real misconception.
  • FN — truly M, model said C: it missed one.
  • FP — truly C, model said M: a false alarm.
  • TN — truly C, model said C: correctly left alone.

Suppose that out of your twenty M items the model caught fourteen, and out of your twenty C items it wrongly flagged three:

Read it as a sentence, not a grid. Twenty responses really contained a misconception; the model found fourteen and walked past six. Twenty were fine; it left seventeen alone and told three students they were wrong when they were not. Rows are the truth you wrote, columns are what the model said, diagonals are agreement.

The two errors are not the same size

The off-diagonal boxes are both mistakes, and here is the point of the whole design: they do not cost the same. A false negative means a student held a belief like "changing my password every month keeps me safe" and the tutor read it and said nothing. The student leaves still believing it, now with a machine's silence as confirmation. Scheduled rotation pushes people towards predictable increments — Summer2026!, Fall2026! — and NIST SP 800-63B recommends against requiring it cited. The misconception survives contact with the tutor. That is the failure that costs something.

A false positive means the tutor corrected a student who was already right. That is genuinely bad — irritating, and enough of it and the student stops trusting the tool, which eventually costs you the true positives too. But nobody's account gets taken over because a tutor was over-cautious.

Unequal costs are why one metric gets to be the headline. Six misses and three false alarms is not the same result as three misses and six false alarms, even though both are nine errors and both give exactly the same accuracy. Accuracy cannot see the difference. Recall can.

4 · The four metrics

All four are arithmetic on those four counts, all with M as the positive class.

Accuracy — of all forty items, what fraction did it get right:

$$\text{accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FN + FP + TN}$$

Precision — of the alarms it raised, what fraction were real. The denominator is everything the model called M:

$$\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}$$

Recall — of the misconceptions that were really there, what fraction it caught. The denominator is everything that truly was M:

$$\text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$$

Those two denominators are the only thing you have to keep straight, and swapping them is the most common error in this whole project. The memory hook: precision is about the model's claims, recall is about the world's misconceptions.

F1 — one number when you want to talk about both. It is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:

$$F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}} = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN}$$

Running the example matrix through all four, with \(TP = 14\), \(FN = 6\), \(FP = 3\), \(TN = 17\):

The arithmetic, in full

accuracy = (14 + 17) / 40 = 31/40 = 0.775

precision = 14 / (14 + 3) = 14/17 = 0.824

recall = 14 / (14 + 6) = 14/20 = 0.700

F1 = 2 × 0.824 × 0.700 / (0.824 + 0.700) = 1.1529 / 1.5235 = 0.757, and the shortcut agrees: 2(14) / (2(14) + 3 + 6) = 28/37 = 0.757.

Positive class M throughout, n = 40. Each of these is a proportion with a wide interval attached at this sample size; section 5 does that part.

Why F1 is a harmonic mean and not an ordinary one

Take a model that flags every single item as M. It catches all twenty real misconceptions, so recall is 100%. It also flags all twenty correct ones, so of its forty alarms half are wrong: precision is 50%. Is that worth deploying? No — it is a system that says "misconception" and has learned nothing. Now score it:

Harmonic against arithmetic proved

Arithmetic mean: (1.00 + 0.50) / 2 = 0.750.

Harmonic mean: 2 × 1.00 × 0.50 / (1.00 + 0.50) = 1.00 / 1.50 = 0.667.

The arithmetic mean gives a useless classifier 0.75, which sounds like a decent result. The harmonic mean gives it 0.667, and drags it further down the more lopsided the pair gets. In general, for any two positive numbers the harmonic mean is at most the arithmetic mean, with equality only when the two are equal. So F1 is pulled towards the smaller of the two, and a model can only score well on it by being decent at both.

A single very high number cannot rescue a single very low one. That is exactly the property you want from a summary score. ∎

F1 is the summary; recall is the headline. Report all four, lead with recall, and say why in the same breath: a missed misconception is the error that costs something.

Lab 1 · Move the errors around and watch the metrics

The design fixes twenty truly-M and twenty truly-C items, so TP + FN = 20 and FP + TN = 20 always, leaving exactly two free numbers. Set them and read the live matrix, the four metrics and their 95% Wilson intervals. Find two settings with the same accuracy and completely different recall — that is the point. Then push false negatives to 20 and watch precision.

Undefined is not zero

Push false negatives to 20 in the lab above. Now TP = 0 and FP = 0, so precision is 0/0: undefined, not zero. The model raised no alarms, so "of its alarms, what fraction were right?" has no answer. G.metrics returns null and G.pct prints undefined, deliberately — printing 0.00 would claim every alarm was wrong, which is a different and false statement. Write "precision is undefined because the model raised no alarms", never "precision was 0".

5 · The floor: what a number has to beat

Your dataset is twenty M and twenty C by construction — ten misconceptions, two M answers and two C answers each. Now consider the most stupid classifier that can be built: it ignores the response entirely and answers M every time. It gets all twenty M items right and all twenty C items wrong, so its accuracy is 20/40 = 50%. It has read nothing and contains no information about passwords whatsoever, and it scores 50%.

The majority-class baseline

The majority-class baseline is the accuracy you get by always guessing whichever class is more common — 50% on a balanced 20/20 set, which is what G.metrics returns as majority. On 90 M and 10 C the floor would be 90%, and "90% accuracy" there would mean precisely nothing.

Every accuracy you report is reported against this floor. Not "the model got 62.5%", but "62.5% against a majority-class floor of 50%, so 12.5 points above chance-level guessing" — and then, immediately, the interval.

This is the single most common way a first machine-learning result turns out to be meaningless. Balancing your set at 20/20 is what makes the floor a clean 50%, and is one of the better reasons to have balanced it.

The floor also has to survive the interval. At n = 40, an accuracy of 24/40 = 60% has a 95% Wilson interval of 44.6% to 73.7%, which contains 50%. So 60% accuracy on forty items is consistent with a model doing nothing at all, and you must not write the word "improved" about it. That is the price of a pilot.

Why Wilson and not the ordinary interval

The textbook formula \(p \pm 1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) fails badly for small n and for proportions near 0 or 1. At 38 correct out of 40 it produces an upper bound of 1.018 — an accuracy of 101.8%, which is not a thing that exists. The Wilson score interval gives 83.5% to 98.6% instead, and never leaves the unit interval by construction. Use G.wilson(k, n) everywhere; the results page depends on it.

6 · Temperature 0, and what reproducible actually means

TEMPERATURE = 0.0 in config.py. Temperature controls how much randomness the model uses when picking each next word. At 1.0 it samples freely and gives a different answer every time; at 0 it takes the most likely option at every step.

The reason is not that deterministic output is better output. It is that you are running a comparison. If the model is random, condition B scoring higher than condition A could just be the dice, and you have no way to tell that apart from a real effect. Temperature 0 removes one source of variation so that the one you introduced on purpose — the prompt — is what is left.

Temperature 0 is not a guarantee observed

Two runs of the same prompt at temperature 0 may still differ slightly. Floating-point addition is not associative, so the order a machine happens to sum things in can flip a near-tie between two candidate words, and library versions, batching and hardware all change that order. One different word early on can change a whole reply.

You will find out how much this matters for your setup, because Phase 4 has you run the whole thing twice and count the items where the two runs disagree. That count tells the reader how much of any difference between conditions could be run-to-run noise: if two identical runs disagree on four items, a two-item difference between conditions means nothing. Report it — "two runs at temperature 0 differed on k of 40 items" is one sentence and costs you nothing.

7 · What would falsify the hypothesis

A study that can only come out one way is not a study. So the prediction gets written down now, before any data exists, in a form specific enough to be wrong:

The prediction, recorded in advance conjectured

The misconception-aware condition will raise recall more than it raises precision.

The reasoning: naming a failure mode helps a model notice that failure mode. It does not obviously help the model avoid crying wolf on a correct answer — if anything, handing it a list of ten things to look for makes it more likely to see one that is not there. So the expected signature is recall up substantially, precision flat or slightly down, F1 up somewhat.

Now the part that makes it a real prediction. Written in advance, here is what would count as the catalogue did not help:

  • Recall barely moves. If recall goes from 55% to 60% that is one extra item out of twenty. The Wilson interval for 11/20 runs 34% to 74% and for 12/20 runs 39% to 78%; those describe the same state of knowledge. The honest sentence is "no detectable difference at this sample size", and it is a finding.
  • Recall rises but precision collapses. If the aware condition simply flags more things — recall 95%, precision 52% — check its total number of M predictions against 40. A model that has learned to say "misconception" more often has not learned to detect misconceptions.
  • Precision rises and recall does not. The opposite of the prediction, and genuinely interesting: the catalogue would be helping it rule things out rather than spot them, and you would have to rewrite the explanation. That is what a surprising result is for.
  • The aware condition is worse. Entirely possible: a 3-billion parameter model given a long list may get distracted, or pattern-match on surface words in the catalogue. "Adding the catalogue reduced recall from 70% to 55%" is a real and useful finding about small models and long prompts.

None of these four outcomes is a failed project. The failed project is the one where you decide afterwards which comparison to report, and writing the prediction down first is what stops you doing that without noticing.

Lab 2 · Cautious or twitchy — the precision/recall tradeoff

Imagine the model ranking all forty responses by how suspicious they look and raising an alarm on the top few. The slider is how many alarms it is willing to raise: 0 is a tutor that never interrupts, 40 is one that flags everything. Watch precision and recall move in opposite directions, find where F1 peaks, and see what reaching 100% recall costs in precision. The ranking is seeded, so the picture is the same on every load.

Two things to take from that picture. Precision and recall genuinely pull against each other: no setting maximises both, so choosing between them is a decision about which error you can afford, not a technical detail. And F1 peaks in the middle rather than at either end — the harmonic mean refusing to reward the tutor that never speaks or the tutor that never shuts up.

Your study does not have this slider; the model gives a verdict, not a ranking. But the same tension is inside it. If the misconception-aware prompt raises recall while precision falls, you have watched the model slide rightwards along this curve, which is a very different thing from the curve itself moving up. Telling those two apart is most of what Phase 5 is about.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1.1 ★arithmetic

A baseline run gives TP = 11, FN = 9, FP = 4, TN = 16. Compute accuracy, precision, recall and F1. State the positive class. Then say, in one sentence, which of the two error types this model makes more of and what that means for a student.

Solution

Positive class is M, "this response contains a misconception". n = 11 + 9 + 4 + 16 = 40, which is the right total, so no item has been lost.

accuracy = (TP + TN)/40 = (11 + 16)/40 = 27/40 = 0.675 = 67.5%.

precision = TP/(TP + FP) = 11/(11 + 4) = 11/15 = 0.733 = 73.3%. Of the fifteen times it raised an alarm, eleven were real.

recall = TP/(TP + FN) = 11/(11 + 9) = 11/20 = 0.550 = 55.0%. Of the twenty misconceptions that were really there, it caught eleven.

F1 = 2TP/(2TP + FP + FN) = 22/(22 + 4 + 9) = 22/35 = 0.629 = 62.9%. Checking against the other form: 2(0.733)(0.550)/(0.733 + 0.550) = 0.8067/1.2833 = 0.629. They agree.

FN = 9 against FP = 4, so it makes more than twice as many misses as false alarms. Nine students in this run held a misconception, wrote it down, and the tutor said nothing — the expensive direction. And note the shape: accuracy 67.5% sounds respectable until you see recall is 55%, barely above the 50% you would get by flipping a coin on every item.

Exercise 1.2 ★★arithmetic

The misconception_aware run on the same forty responses gives TP = 17, FN = 3, FP = 7, TN = 13. Compute the same four metrics, then compare them with Exercise 1.1. Does the change match the prediction recorded in section 7? Then compute the Wilson intervals for the two recall figures — 11/20 is 34.2% to 74.2%, and 17/20 is 64.0% to 94.8% — and say what that does to your confidence.

Solution

Positive class M, n = 17 + 3 + 7 + 13 = 40.

accuracy = (17 + 13)/40 = 30/40 = 0.750 = 75.0%.

precision = 17/(17 + 7) = 17/24 = 0.708 = 70.8%.

recall = 17/(17 + 3) = 17/20 = 0.850 = 85.0%.

F1 = 2(17)/(2(17) + 7 + 3) = 34/44 = 0.773 = 77.3%.

Side by side, baseline → aware:

accuracy 67.5% → 75.0% (up 7.5 points)
precision 73.3% → 70.8% (down 2.5 points)
recall 55.0% → 85.0% (up 30.0 points)
F1 62.9% → 77.3% (up 14.4 points)

This is exactly the predicted signature: a large gain in recall, a small loss in precision. The misses fell from nine to three; the false alarms rose from four to seven. In tutor terms, the catalogue turned six silences into six corrections, at the cost of three extra unnecessary corrections. Given that missing is the error that costs something, that is a trade worth making, and you should say so in exactly those words rather than saying "F1 improved".

Now the humility. The two recall intervals are 34.2%–74.2% and 64.0%–94.8%. They overlap, between about 64% and 74%. Overlapping intervals are not a formal test of the difference — a proper test of a paired difference is a separate calculation, and these two runs are paired, since it is the same forty items both times. But the overlap does tell you the honest register to write in. "Recall rose by 30 points (55% to 85%), though at n = 20 positive items the intervals for the two figures overlap; this is a suggestive pilot result, not an established effect" is a sentence a reader will believe. "The catalogue improves recall" is not.

Exercise 1.3 ★★thinking

A classmate reports: "My system got 100% recall. It never missed a single misconception." Their matrix is TP = 20, FN = 0, FP = 20, TN = 0. Compute all four metrics and say whether this is a good result. Then say what this implies about leading with recall.

Solution

accuracy = (20 + 0)/40 = 50.0%. precision = 20/(20 + 20) = 20/40 = 50.0%. recall = 20/(20 + 0) = 100.0%. F1 = 2(1.00)(0.50)/(1.50) = 66.7%.

The recall claim is true and completely worthless. TN = 0 gives it away: the system never once said C. It answers M to everything, which is precisely the always-guess-M classifier from section 5. Its accuracy is 50.0%, sitting exactly on the majority-class floor, so it contains no information about passwords at all. You could replace it with a piece of paper reading "M".

Two lessons. First, recall alone can be gamed by anything willing to say yes, so "recall is the headline metric" never means "recall is the only metric". You report all four and lead with recall; you do not report recall and hide the rest. Second, this is why the specificity and the raw counts matter: a glance at TN = 0, or at accuracy sitting on the floor, kills the claim instantly, while the recall figure on its own does not.

Practically: when your own aware condition shows a recall jump in Phase 5, the first thing to check is how many M predictions it made in total. If it is close to 40, you have this classifier, not a result.

Exercise 1.4 ★★thinking

Here is a result: "The misconception-aware condition reached 60% accuracy on 40 items, compared with 50% for baseline." Is it good? Write the two or three sentences you would actually put in a write-up. The Wilson interval for 24/40 is 44.6% to 73.7%; for 20/40 it is 35.2% to 64.8%.

Solution

It is not a result yet. Three things are wrong with the sentence as written.

1. The floor is unstated, and it happens to be 50%. The baseline condition's 50% accuracy is exactly what you get by ignoring the input and always answering M. So the comparison being offered is "10 points above a classifier that does nothing", which is a much smaller claim than it sounds.

2. The interval swallows the difference. 24/40 has a 95% Wilson interval of 44.6% to 73.7%. That interval contains 50%. A model whose true accuracy is chance-level would produce 24 correct out of 40 quite often. So the data are consistent with no effect at all.

3. Accuracy is the wrong headline here anyway. Accuracy cannot tell a run with 9 misses and 4 false alarms from one with 4 misses and 9 false alarms, and those are different tutors. The interesting number is recall.

What to write instead: "The misconception-aware condition reached 60.0% accuracy (95% Wilson CI 44.6–73.7%) against a majority-class floor of 50%, which the interval contains; the baseline reached 50.0% (35.2–64.8%). At n = 40 this design cannot distinguish a ten-point accuracy difference from noise. Recall, reported below, is the more informative comparison."

Then in the limitations: say how many items would be needed to detect a difference this size, and note that this is a pilot whose job was to establish the method.

Exercise 1.5 ★★★research

Section 2 admitted a weakness: prompt B differs from prompt A in two ways, not one — it has the catalogue, and its instruction sentence points at the catalogue. Design a third condition that separates those two differences. Say exactly what it contains, and write down in advance what each possible outcome would let you conclude.

Solution

The condition: a placebo catalogue. Call it irrelevant_catalogue. It is byte-for-byte identical to misconception_aware, including the "decide whether it reflects any of the misconceptions listed above" sentence, except that the ten statements listed are documented misconceptions from a completely different domain — say, mechanics: "heavier objects fall faster", "a moving object needs a continuous force to keep moving", and so on. Ten of them, matched in length and in register to your real ten.

The logic: this condition has everything the aware condition has — the same length, the same structure, the same instruction sentence, the same "here is a list, check against it" framing — except the one thing that is supposed to matter, which is that the list describes the misconceptions actually present in the data.

Outcomes, written down in advance:

Aware > irrelevant ≈ baseline. The gain came from the content of the catalogue. This is the result you are hoping for and it is now defensible, because the wording change and the extra prompt length have been controlled for.

Aware ≈ irrelevant > baseline. Any list helps. The model is not using your ten misconceptions — it is responding to being told to look for problems, which primes it to answer M more often. Your finding changes completely: it becomes a finding about prompt framing, not about misconception knowledge. Check whether the M-prediction count rose in both.

Aware ≈ irrelevant ≈ baseline. Nothing helps at this model size. Report it. This is a real result about 3-billion parameter models.

Irrelevant > aware. Something odd is happening; suspect a bug before a discovery — check that the catalogue is loading, that conditions are not swapped, and that the parser is not failing differently across conditions.

Cost: one more condition means forty more model calls, roughly doubling to tripling a run that already takes an afternoon. That is affordable, which is why this is the right variant to propose. Adding it is a change to CONDITIONS in config.py, a new builder in prompts.py, and a branch in build_prompt — the file is laid out to make exactly this easy. Do it only after the two-condition study runs end to end.

Exercise 1.6 ★★by hand

A run comes back with the model having answered C to all forty items: TP = 0, FN = 20, FP = 0, TN = 20. Compute all four metrics, being careful about what is defined. Then write the one sentence you would put in the results section, and say which of the two errors this model makes.

Solution

accuracy = (0 + 20)/40 = 50.0% — exactly the majority-class floor, as it must be, since this is the always-guess-C classifier and the set is balanced.

recall = 0/(0 + 20) = 0/20 = 0.0%. This one is zero and is defined: there were twenty misconceptions to find and it found none.

precision = 0/(0 + 0) = 0/0 = undefined. Not zero. The model raised no alarms at all, so "of its alarms, what fraction were correct?" has no subject. G.metrics returns null here and G.pct prints undefined.

F1 = undefined, because it is built from precision. In the shortcut form 2TP/(2TP + FP + FN) you would get 0/20 = 0, which is why the code computes it from precision and recall instead: the shortcut quietly hides a division that has no answer.

The sentence: "The model returned C for all 40 items. Accuracy was 50.0%, exactly the majority-class floor; recall was 0.0%; precision and F1 are undefined, as no positive predictions were made."

Every error it makes is a false negative — twenty misses, zero false alarms. It is the perfectly silent tutor, which is the worst possible tutor for this purpose while looking respectable on accuracy. If you see this in a real run, suspect the prompt or the parser before you conclude anything about the model: a parser returning C where it should return ? produces exactly this pattern.

Exercise 1.7 ★★writing

Write, in your own words and before any data exists, the paragraph that goes in your write-up under the heading "What would have changed our conclusion". It must name a specific numerical outcome for at least two of the four metrics. Keep it under 120 words. Then put a date on it and commit it.

Solution

There is no single right answer, but a good one is specific enough to be checked against later. For example:

"We predicted before running that the misconception-aware condition would raise recall substantially while leaving precision flat or slightly lower. We would have concluded the catalogue did not help if recall had risen by two items or fewer out of twenty (a change of 10 percentage points or less, well inside the Wilson intervals at this n), or if the gain in recall had come with the aware condition predicting M on more than about 32 of the 40 items, since that would indicate a drift towards the always-guess-M classifier rather than improved detection. We would also have reported a reduction in recall, which is a plausible outcome for a 3B model given a long prompt."

Check yours against three things. Does it name numbers, not adjectives? Does it describe at least one outcome that would have embarrassed you? Could a stranger check, from your results table alone, whether the condition was met? If yes to all three, it does its job.

Commit it before Phase 4 runs. The whole value of a pre-registered prediction is the timestamp; written afterwards it is just a description of what happened.

Going deeper (optional)

Specificity. G.metrics also returns spec = TN/(TN + FP) — recall's mirror image, computed with C as the positive class. Reading a result twice, once each way, catches a swapped denominator fast.

The curve in Lab 2 has a name. Sweeping a decision threshold and plotting precision against recall gives a precision–recall curve; plotting recall against the false positive rate gives an ROC curve, whose area is a threshold-free summary of a ranking's quality cited. Your model outputs a verdict rather than a score, so you cannot draw either from this design — but if you ever get it to emit a confidence, you can, and that would be a genuinely stronger version of this study.
