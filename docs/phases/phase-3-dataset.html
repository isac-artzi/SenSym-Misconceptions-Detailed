Forty responses, and the trap in them

Writing the only part of this project nobody else can write for you, in a way that can actually measure something.

Everything upstream of here is installation and typing. This is the phase where the study is either made or quietly destroyed, and the destruction is silent: the numbers come out looking excellent, both conditions score in the nineties, and the experiment has measured nothing but your own writing habits. This page is mostly about not doing that. Budget two weeks, and expect to throw some items away.

By the end of this phase you can

  • Explain what a ceiling effect is, and say out loud why it would end this study.
  • Write a misconception item that is realistic, plausible, and about exactly one idea, and recognise a "hard" one — correct-sounding but wrong, or wrong-sounding but correct.
  • Justify 20 M and 20 C, and state the majority-class baseline that balance buys you.
  • Run the ten-item probe on your first ten items, and read a perfect score as a stop rather than as a pass.
  • Run a blind second labelling, collect the one sentence that says what decided each label, and explain why agreement is U-shaped rather than a floor.
  • Compute percentage agreement and Cohen's kappa from a 2×2 table, and say which of the two belongs at the gate and which belongs in the write-up.
  • Fill in responses.csv and misconceptions.csv without the comma trap eating a row, and decide with a stated rule rather than a feeling whether the dataset is ready for Phase 4.

1 · The trap this page exists to prevent

Here is the failure, written out plainly so you can recognise it before it happens.

You sit down to write twenty responses containing a misconception. Because you know which ones are supposed to be wrong, you write them wrong. They fill up with always, never, impossible, 100% safe. Then you write twenty correct responses, and because you know those are supposed to be right, they come out careful and hedged: it depends, generally, although. Every M item shouts and every C item murmurs.

Now run the study. The baseline prompt scores 97%. The misconception-aware prompt scores 97%. You have not discovered that the catalogue does not help. You have discovered that a three-billion-parameter model can tell shouting from murmuring, which was never in doubt. The two conditions cannot differ, because there is no room above 97% for the better one to occupy. That is a ceiling effect, and it is the single most common way a small study like this produces a month of work and no finding.

The rule to internalise

If your model scores near 100% under both prompts, suspect your dataset before you celebrate. An experiment can only detect a difference in the space between the floor and the ceiling. Your floor is fixed at 50% by the design (section 4). Your ceiling is set entirely by how hard your items are, and that is the one thing you control this phase.

Lab · the ceiling-effect simulator

One slider: how obviously different your twenty M items are from your twenty C items. Push it right and both prompts climb toward 100%. Watch the shaded band — the space between the two conditions, which is the only thing your experiment can measure — get squeezed out of existence. Read the last line of the readout at each setting. It says what you would be entitled to conclude.

Two things in that picture are worth saying in words. First, the intervals are enormous: at n = 40 a 95% Wilson interval is roughly twenty-five percentage points wide in the middle of the range, so the honest question is never "did the aware prompt win" but "did it win by enough to see through that". Second, two intervals overlapping does not by itself mean there is no difference — both conditions run over the same forty items, so Phase 5's comparison is paired and more sensitive than eyeballing two intervals. None of that helps if the gap is zero. No test can find a difference your dataset made impossible.

2 · What a good item looks like

Five properties. Each one has a test you can apply in about five seconds.

PropertyThe test
RealisticCould a real tenth-grader have written this in a real class? Read it aloud. If it sounds like a textbook or like a cartoon, it fails.
PlausibleDoes the wrong one contain a grain of truth pushed too far? A misconception people actually hold is never absurd — that is why they hold it.
A student's voiceFirst person, contractions, a specific detail. "My password is 24 characters" beats "Passwords should be long".
One misconceptionIf an item contains two errors, and the model catches one, is that M? You will not be able to answer consistently across forty items, so do not create the problem.
Long enough to reason aboutRoughly one to three sentences. Under a sentence there is nothing to detect; over three you are testing reading comprehension instead.

The twelve demo items in python/data/sample/responses.csv are written to that standard, so they are a useful target even though no number computed from them is ever a result. Here are four of them beside a weak rewrite of the same idea — the version you will write by accident at eleven at night with nine items left to go.

TruthWeak — do not write thisGood — the demo set's versionWhat the weak one leaks
M "A really long password can never ever be cracked, it is 100% impossible." "My password is 24 characters so it's basically uncrackable. Length is the only thing that really matters for password strength." (S01) Three absolute words in one sentence. A keyword matcher wins without reading.
C "Password strength is a function of entropy over the character set, per NIST SP 800-63B." "Length helps a lot, but a long password made of common words can still be guessed by a dictionary attack. It needs to be long and unpredictable." (S03) Nobody in your grade talks like that. The register alone marks it C.
M "Changing your password always makes you completely safe, guaranteed." "Our school makes us reset our password every 60 days, which is the main reason school accounts are more secure than personal ones." (S06) The weak one is a slogan. The good one is a belief a real person holds for a real reason.
C "MFA is good but not perfect." "Two-factor is the single best thing I've turned on, though I switched from SMS codes to an authenticator app because SMS can be intercepted." (S12) Six words is not enough to reason about. There is no claim there to be right or wrong about.
Two automated ways to detect this, both of which failed observed

It would be very convenient if a script could read your forty items and tell you whether they are separable by surface features. Two such scripts were built for this project and both were thrown away. The failures are recorded here so that nobody builds them a third time, and because between them they are the reason the gate in section 9 is human rather than statistical.

Attempt one: use the mock model as a keyword detector. The fake model in --mock mode is not a model at all. Open python/misconception/llm_client.py and read ask_llm_mock: it says M if the text contains one of nine words — always, never, impossible, guarantee, completely, definitely, can't be, cannot be, safe from — and then flips its own verdict whenever

$$(7L + 13E) \bmod 10 \;\lt\; 3,$$

where $L$ is the length of the response in characters and $E$ is how many letter e's it contains. That flip is a function of the shape of the text, not its meaning, and it is what breaks the idea. On a deliberately keyword-separable eight-item set — every M carrying an absolute, every C hedging — the mock scored 6/8 = 0.750. Appending trailing spaces to every item, which changes no word in any of them and therefore changes nothing at all about separability, moved that score around between 0.500 and 0.875. A deliberately tell-free set of the same size wandered between 0.375 and 0.625 under the same treatment. The two ranges overlap almost entirely. A diagnostic whose reading changes when you add whitespace is not measuring what you wanted it to measure.

Attempt two: train an actual classifier. A TF-IDF logistic regression under four-fold cross-validation, asked to predict M or C from the words alone. If it succeeds, your items are word-separable and you are in trouble. On the twelve demo items it came out between 0.417 and 0.500 depending on the split, with individual folds at 0.333 — at or below the 50% chance level, which is what "learned nothing" looks like. That is not evidence the demo items are clean; it is evidence that nine training items per fold is too few to learn from. Forty items would give it thirty, which is barely better.

The conclusion to carry forward. There is no statistical separability test worth trusting at $n = 40$. What replaces it is two cheap human-and-model things: the ten-item probe in section 8, which asks the real model directly, and the second reader's sentence about what decided each label, in section 6. Running --mock on your own data is still worth doing — it proves the forty rows load and the counts are right — but its accuracy is not a signal about your dataset and must not be read as one.

3 · The hard cases are the only valuable ones

An easy item is one where both prompts get it right. It contributes to both accuracies equally and to the difference between them not at all. Forty easy items produce two identical numbers and no study. The entire signal in this experiment lives in the items that one prompt gets and the other does not, and those are, necessarily, the hard ones. Aim for roughly a third of your set to be items you had to think about yourself.

There are two kinds, and you want both.

A wrong response that sounds right

"Our school makes us reset our password every 60 days, which is the main reason school accounts are more secure than personal ones." (S06, truth = M)

Calm, institutional, no absolutes, and completely mistaken. Scheduled rotation is the belief being tested: NIST SP 800-63B recommends against requiring periodic password changes, because forced rotation pushes people toward predictable increments and does nothing if the credential has already leaked. cited A model that detects this has done something a keyword matcher cannot. A model that misses it produces a false negative, and false negatives are what recall is about.

A correct response that sounds wrong

"I only change a password if there's a reason to — a breach notice or a login I don't recognize. Rotating on a schedule just makes people pick predictable patterns." (S07, truth = C)

Read quickly, this sounds like a student defending laziness, and a naive reader marks it M. It is right, and for the right reason. This item is the mirror of S06 and belongs in the set precisely because it punishes a model that has learned "mentions not changing passwords → M" instead of learning the actual claim. Items like this are where a false positive comes from, and false positives are what precision is about.

Both of those are hard. Neither is unlabelable, and the difference between those two words is the whole of section 6. Here is one that crosses the line:

Hard, versus impossible — do not confuse them

"I write all my passwords in a notebook I keep in a drawer at home, and they're all different."

Is that M or C? It depends entirely on the threat model. Against a remote attacker it is fine and arguably good practice; against someone with physical access to the house it is not. There is no defensible single label, so a second reader will disagree with you no matter what you choose, and the item measures the coin-flip rather than the model.

Hard means a thoughtful reader agrees with you after thinking. Impossible means a thoughtful reader disagrees with you after thinking. Keep the first, cut the second. If you love an item like this, the fix is to add the missing context to the item itself — make the student mention the threat they have in mind — not to argue for your label.

4 · Balance, and why exactly twenty and twenty

Twenty M and twenty C, four responses for each of ten misconceptions: two M and two C per misconception. That structure is doing two separate jobs, and both are worth being able to defend out loud.

Job one: it fixes the floor. The dumbest possible model ignores the text entirely and always guesses whichever label is more common. Its accuracy is the majority-class baseline:

$$\text{majority baseline} \;=\; \frac{\max(n_M,\, n_C)}{n_M + n_C}.$$

With 20 and 20 that is exactly $20/40 = 0.5$. Every number you report afterwards can be read against a floor of 50% that a reader does not have to be told twice. Let it drift to 28 M and 12 C and the floor moves to $28/40 = 70\%$: a model scoring 68% is now performing worse than a rock, and an unwary reader who sees "68% accuracy" thinks it did fine. load_responses() in run_experiment.py prints a warning when $|n_M - n_C| > 0.3n$ for exactly this reason, but a warning is not a substitute for getting it right.

Job two: it stops the topic from doing the work. This is the subtler one. Suppose all your password-length items are M and all your MFA items are C. A model can then score brilliantly by recognising the topic and never engaging with the claim, and you would have no way to tell that apart from real detection. Putting two M and two C on every misconception means the topic carries no information about the label, so the only thing left to distinguish them is the thing you actually care about.

The positive class, stated once and used everywhere

Throughout this project the positive class is M: "this response contains a misconception." Every metric is with respect to it.

$$\text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}.$$

Recall is the headline: of the misconceptions that really were there, what fraction did the model catch. A tutor that stays silent while a student holds a dangerous belief has done the expensive kind of damage. If a denominator is zero the metric is undefined, not zero — G.metrics returns null and prints "undefined", and you should write it that way too.

5 · Label them yourself, cold

Before you ask anyone else, do it yourself, at least a week after writing them, without looking at your labels. This is not a formality. An item you cannot re-label correctly from memory is an item nobody can label.

Lab · the labelling drill

Twelve real items from python/data/sample/responses.csv, shuffled, one at a time. Decide M or C before you look at the recorded label. The matrix scores you against that label — the headings say "model said" because for the next two minutes you are the classifier, which is exactly the comparison Phase 5 runs on llama3.2:3b. Notice which items you get wrong. Those are the good ones.

6 · The second reader, and how much you agreed

You are the worst possible judge of whether your own labels are obvious, because you wrote them and you remember what you meant. So hand a subset to somebody else — a friend, a parent, a sibling, the mentor — and have them label it blind.

How to run it properly

Take at least 20 of your 40 items, ideally with equal numbers of your M and your C. Strip the ground_truth column. Give them the misconception catalogue and one sentence of instruction: "mark M if this response contains one of these misconceptions, C if it does not." Do not sit next to them. Do not explain any item.

Then ask for one more thing, and this is the part that does the real work: for every item, one sentence saying what decided it. Not a justification of the label, just a note of what they looked at. It costs them a few extra minutes and it is the single most informative thing you will collect this phase, because a column of sentences reading "it said never", "it said guaranteed", "the other one hedged" is a ceiling effect caught red-handed, and no agreement statistic would have caught it. A column reading "had to think about whether rotation actually helps here" is the opposite, and is the thing you are hoping for.

If a human cannot reliably agree with you, the model has no chance, and the problem is the label rather than the model. That sentence is the reason this step exists, and it is worth a line in your write-up whichever way it comes out.

Percentage agreement

The simple version, and a perfectly respectable thing to report:

$$p_o \;=\; \frac{\text{items you both gave the same label}}{\text{items you both labelled}}.$$

Say you both label 20 items and agree on 17. Then $p_o = 17/20 = 0.85$, and you write "the second rater agreed with my label on 17 of 20 items (85%)". Nothing wrong with that. Two weaknesses are worth knowing about before you lean on the number.

The first is that it flatters you: with two labels, two people guessing at random agree about half the time, so 85% is really only most of the way from 50 to 100, not from 0 to 100. Cohen's kappa below is the standard fix for that.

The second is that it is imprecise, and kappa does not fix that at all. The 95% Wilson interval on 17/20 runs from 64.0% to 94.8% — a span of nearly thirty-one percentage points. The same 17 agreements are compatible with a true agreement rate of about 64% and with one of about 95%. That is why section 9's rule is a band rather than a threshold: a cutoff drawn anywhere inside an interval that wide is a cutoff you could hit or miss by rewriting one item, which is an invitation to rewrite one item.

Cohen's kappa, for the write-up cited

Everything from here to the end of this section belongs in your methods and results section, not at the gate. It is worth knowing, it is worth reporting, and at $n = 20$ it is not worth deciding on — for the same reason the raw percentage is not: the interval around it is far too wide to separate a dataset that passes from one that does not.

What kappa does is subtract off the agreement you would expect by chance and rescale what is left, so that 0 means "no better than chance" and 1 means "perfect". cited (Cohen, 1960, A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales, Educational and Psychological Measurement 20(1), 37–46.)

$$\kappa \;=\; \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}, \qquad p_e \;=\; ab + (1-a)(1-b),$$

where $a$ is the fraction of items you called M and $b$ is the fraction the second reader called M. The $p_e$ term is the probability two people would land on the same label by luck alone, given how often each of them says M.

A shortcut that is exactly true for a balanced subset proved

If your half of the subset is exactly balanced — you labelled as many M as C, so $a = \tfrac{1}{2}$ — then

$$p_e = \tfrac{1}{2}b + \tfrac{1}{2}(1-b) = \tfrac{1}{2} \qquad\text{whatever the second reader does,}$$

and substituting into the definition gives

$$\kappa = \frac{p_o - \tfrac12}{1 - \tfrac12} = 2p_o - 1.$$

So on a balanced subset, 90% agreement is $\kappa = 0.8$, 85% is $0.7$, 80% is $0.6$, and 70% is $0.4$. Two lines of algebra, and it means you can sanity-check any kappa you compute in your head. It also shows why kappa is the more honest number: 70% agreement sounds respectable and $\kappa = 0.4$ does not, and $\kappa = 0.4$ is telling the truth.

Worked, on ten items

You and your reader both label 10 items. You called 5 of them M; they called 4 M. You agree on 9 (both said M on 4 items, both said C on 5), and on the tenth you said M and they said C.

$p_o = 9/10 = 0.9$.   $a = 5/10 = 0.5$,   $b = 4/10 = 0.4$.

$p_e = (0.5)(0.4) + (0.5)(0.6) = 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.50$.

$\kappa = (0.9 - 0.5)/(1 - 0.5) = 0.4/0.5 = \mathbf{0.80}$ — which is what the shortcut above predicts, since $2(0.9) - 1 = 0.8$.

The conventional reading of kappa is Landis and Koch's: 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1.00 almost perfect. cited (Landis & Koch, 1977, Biometrics 33(1), 159–174.) Those bands are convention, not mathematics — nothing computes them, people agreed on them — so quote them as a convention and not as a law. Report your kappa alongside the raw agreement and the number of items it was computed on, and resist the urge to attach a verdict to it. On 20 items it is a description, not a decision.

If kappa is one step too far

Report raw percentage agreement and say so plainly: "a second rater labelled 20 of the 40 responses without seeing my labels; we agreed on 17 (85%, 95% Wilson interval 64.0% to 94.8%)." That is honest, checkable, and more than most work at this level does. What is not acceptable is skipping the second reader and asserting that the labels are obvious. The number is small; the absence of the number is not.

The result you must not misread

Perfect agreement is not automatically good news. If your reader agrees on 20 of 20 and says it was easy, you have evidence for the section 1 problem, not against it. This is the whole logic of the gate in section 9, and it is the least intuitive idea on this page: high agreement can mean your labels are clean, and it can equally mean your items are giving themselves away.

The sentences settle it. Ask the second question — "which ones did you have to think about?" — and read the "what decided it" column. If the answers are "none of them" and "it said never", go back and make some items harder. Write their answers down; they are data, and they go in the write-up.

7 · The two files, and the comma that eats them

Both files already exist in python/data/ with placeholder rows starting <. The loaders drop any row whose text field still begins with that character, so a half-finished file behaves sensibly instead of crashing.

misconceptions.csv — exactly 10 rows, four columns:

misconception_id,statement,why_wrong,real_world_consequence

responses.csv — exactly 40 rows, five columns:

response_id,misconception_id,ground_truth,response_text,notes
ColumnWhat goes in it
response_idR01 to R40. Must be unique; the loader stops on duplicates.
misconception_idM01–M10, matching a row in misconceptions.csv.
ground_truthCapital M or capital C, nothing else, no trailing space. This column is your entire experiment.
response_textThe student's answer, one to three sentences. The only field the model ever sees.
notesFor you. Why you labelled it that way, what made it hard, who else read it. The pipeline never reads this column, so write freely — and your Phase 5 self will thank you.
Save as CSV, not xlsx, and mind the commas

Writing these in Excel or Google Sheets is the right move: type normally, and the quoting is handled for you on export. But you must File → Save As → CSV. An .xlsx sitting in python/data/ is invisible to the loader, which opens responses.csv by name and will tell you the file is not there.

If you type a row by hand in a text editor, any field containing a comma has to be wrapped in double quotes. Miss one and you get a row with too many fields:

R08,M02,C,I only change a password if there is a reason to, like a breach notice.,

which pandas counts as six fields where the header promised five:

pandas.errors.ParserError: Error tokenizing data. C error: Expected 5 fields in line 9, saw 6

The fix is one pair of quotes:

R08,M02,C,"I only change a password if there is a reason to, like a breach notice.",

The nastier version of this bug does not crash. If the stray comma is in the first data row, pandas can quietly shift every field one column left, so ground_truth ends up holding a fragment of somebody's sentence. That is why load_responses() checks every label against {M, C} and stops with the offending response_id rather than running eighty model calls on garbage.

8 · The ten-item probe

This is the single highest-leverage two minutes in the project. Everything above is advice about how to write items. This is the one step that measures whether the advice worked, and it does it while there is still time to act on the answer.

Write ten items first. Not forty — ten. Five M and five C: two of your misconceptions complete at four items each, plus one M and one C belonging to a third. Put them in responses.csv as rows R01 to R10, with those three misconceptions filled in properly in misconceptions.csv. Then, before you write the other thirty, hand those ten to the real model under both prompts.

$ cd python
$ python3 misconception/run_experiment.py --probe 10

Twenty calls — ten items under each of the two prompts — which on a laptop is about two minutes. --probe N takes the first N rows of your file and stops there; there is no --mock and no --sample on that line, because the whole point is to hear from llama3.2:3b itself rather than from a keyword rule.

A perfect score is a stop, not a pass

Read this before you run it, so the result cannot be reinterpreted afterwards. If the model scores 10/10 under both prompts, that is a failure of the dataset and you stop writing items. It means your ten items are separable without any help at all, so the catalogue has nothing left to contribute and both conditions will sit at the ceiling — the far right-hand end of Lab 1, arrived at two weeks early and for free.

The response is not to celebrate and write thirty more in the same style. It is to go back and write harder ones: an M that reads as reasonable and hedged, a C that reads as overconfident. Then probe again. Finding this now costs you ten items. Finding it in Phase 5 costs you the study.

Here is what the run prints. This is the shape you do not want, produced on the demo data with the fake model so that the block below is a real terminal transcript rather than an invention:

Those two blocks are mock output, and mock output only shows the shape

--mock and --sample are there so you can see what the probe looks like before you have written anything, and for no other reason. The fake model ignores the prompt it was handed, so the two conditions always come out identical under --mock; and its 3/6 is a fact about nine keywords and the length of six sentences, not about the demo items. Your probe runs the real model on your own ten items, with no flags: --probe 10 and nothing else.

One thing the probe does not check: a score of 3/10 is not a pass either. The probe is built to catch the ceiling, which is the failure that ends the study, and it says nothing about the opposite end of Lab 1 — items so ambiguous that nobody, human or model, can label them. That end is what the second reader in section 6 is for. The two checks catch different failures and you need both.

Why it deliberately prints no metrics

You may have noticed what is missing. There is no accuracy, no recall, no confusion matrix, no Wilson interval — just two fractions and a verdict in words. That is on purpose, and _probe_verdict() in run_experiment.py says so in its docstring.

Ten items that you chose, wrote and labelled yourself are not a sample of anything. A recall figure computed on them would be a number about your own writing, dressed up in the vocabulary of a result — and a number that exists gets reported. Someone will put it in a slide, and then in a sentence, and by the time it reaches a reader nobody remembers it came from ten items the author wrote. Printing "3/6 correct" and a paragraph of English instead of "recall = 0.60" closes that path off at the source. The probe is a go / no-go signal about the dataset, and turning it into a metric would make it something else.

9 · The gate

Rebuilding here is a normal outcome, not a failure, and it is far cheaper than rebuilding after two runs and a written-up analysis. It usually means rewriting a handful of items, not starting over.

The gate has one primary criterion and five supporting ones. The primary criterion is the probe in section 8, because it is the only one that asks the actual model the actual question. The supporting criteria are checks on the thing the probe cannot see: whether the labels themselves hold up.

The counterintuitive part: agreement is U-shaped, not a floor

The obvious way to write this gate would be "the second reader must agree with you on at least 85% of items". That rule is worse than no rule, and it is worth understanding exactly why, because the same mistake is available in every part of this project.

Consider the dataset from section 1 — the one where every M item contains never and every C item hedges. Two labellers will agree on essentially all of it. There is nothing to disagree about: the label is written on the surface of each item in plain sight. So that dataset scores 100% agreement. It is also the dataset that kills the study stone dead, because a model with no help whatsoever already separates it.

A one-sided threshold would therefore award its highest score to the most dangerous dataset you can build. Agreement is not a floor to clear; it is a diagnostic with a failure mode at each end. Too low and your labels mean nothing. Too high and your items are giving themselves away. What you want is the middle: a dataset where a careful second reader agrees with you most of the time, and has to stop and think in order to do it.

Agreement on the 20 blind-labelled itemsWhat it meansWhat you do
Below 70%
(under 14 of 20)		 The labels are the problem, not the model. Two people cannot agree what these items mean, so any model score would be measuring the disagreement. Stop. Do not run Phase 4. Go through the items out loud with the reader, then rewrite the ambiguous ones or drop the misconception they belong to.
70% to 90%
(14 to 18 of 20)		 The target band. Real labels, real difficulty. Some items required thought and one or two went the other way. Proceed — once every single disagreement has been discussed to a resolution. Report the pre-resolution figure in the write-up, not the post-resolution one.
Above 90%
(19 or 20 of 20)		 A warning, not a pass. Either your labels are unusually clean or your items are advertising their answers, and the number alone cannot tell you which. Read the "what decided it" sentences and run the keyword audit below. Clean probe plus clean audit: acceptable. Perfect probe: your items are too easy, go back to section 8.

The band is deliberately wide because the numbers underneath it are imprecise. The 95% Wilson intervals on the three boundary cases are [48.1%, 85.5%] for 14/20, [64.0%, 94.8%] for 17/20 and [76.4%, 99.1%] for 19/20. Those overlap heavily. A sharp threshold drawn inside intervals that wide is a threshold you could cross by rewriting one item — which is precisely the invitation you do not want to hand yourself at the moment you are deciding whether your own dataset is good enough.

The keyword audit — crude, and it works at n = 40

Take the five absolutes: always, never, impossible, guaranteed, 100%. Search your response_text column for each, and write down two counts: how many of your 20 M items contain at least one, and how many of your 20 C items do.

Lopsided counts are the commonest tell there is. Eleven M items and one C item means the word never is doing a job in your dataset that you intended the misconception to do. Roughly equal counts — or near-zero on both sides — means the vocabulary is not carrying the label. Do this by eye in the spreadsheet; it takes five minutes and needs no code.

It is a crude instrument and it only catches one family of tells. It is also the only automated check on this page that survived contact with $n = 40$, which is more than can be said for the two in section 2. If the counts come out lopsided, the fix is not to delete the words: it is to move some of them into C items where they are used correctly — "no password is 100% safe, which is why I also turned on two-factor" — so that the presence of an absolute stops predicting the label.

10 · Exercises

Exercise 3.1 ★★writing

Take misconception M02, "changing passwords often makes accounts safer." Write one hard M item and one hard C item for it. Hard means: a thoughtful reader will get it right, but only after stopping. Then write one sentence for each saying what makes it hard.

Solution

Hard M: "I keep the same base password but add the month on the end and change it every 30 days, so even if one gets leaked it's already out of date by the time anyone tries it."

What makes it hard: there are no absolutes anywhere in it, the student sounds thoughtful and organised, and the sentence contains a real security concept (a leaked credential going stale). The error is buried in the mechanism — a predictable increment is trivially guessable once one version leaks, so the rotation buys almost nothing. A keyword matcher sees "leaked", "changed", "30 days" and has no basis for a verdict.

Hard C: "I haven't changed my email password in two years and I'm not planning to. It's long, random, only used on that one site, and nothing has shown up in a breach check."

What makes it hard: it sounds like textbook negligence, and it opens with the exact behaviour a naive reader has been taught to flag. It is correct: with a unique, high-entropy credential and no evidence of compromise, scheduled rotation adds no security, which is the position NIST SP 800-63B takes. cited

Notice that this pair is genuinely mirrored: the M item changes its password and is wrong, the C item refuses to and is right. That mirroring is what stops the model from scoring by keyword, and it is the thing to aim for on all ten misconceptions.

Exercise 3.2 ★arithmetic

You run out of time and submit 28 M items and 12 C items. (a) What is the majority-class baseline? (b) A model that outputs M for every single item without reading it — what are its accuracy, recall and precision, with M as the positive class? (c) Why is this a problem for the write-up even if your model scores 75%?

Solution

(a) $\max(28,12)/40 = 28/40 = 0.70$, so the floor is 70%.

(b) Always-M gives $TP = 28$, $FN = 0$, $FP = 12$, $TN = 0$. Accuracy $= (28+0)/40 = 70\%$. Recall $= 28/(28+0) = 100\%$ — it catches every misconception, by refusing to ever say C. Precision $= 28/(28+12) = 70\%$. A model with perfect recall that has learned nothing at all.

(c) Because 75% is now only five points above a rock, and you have to say so. Worse, the headline metric of this study is recall, and the degenerate model has perfect recall — so on an unbalanced set a high recall no longer distinguishes a good model from a broken one. Balance is what keeps recall meaningful. With 20 and 20, always-M gives accuracy 50%, recall 100%, precision 50%, and the 50% precision immediately gives it away.

Exercise 3.3 ★★thinking

Your second reader blind-labels 20 items and, as asked, writes one sentence per item on what decided it. Decide, for each case, what you do next and why. (a) They agree on 20 of 20, it took five minutes, and half the sentences say some version of "it used an absolute word". (b) They agree on 17 of 20. (c) They agree on 12 of 20. (d) They agree on 19 of 20, the sentences describe the claims rather than the wording, and the single disagreement is an item you already suspected was ambiguous.

Solution

(a) 20/20, easy, sentences about wording. 100% is above the 90% line, so it is a warning rather than a pass — and here the warning is confirmed by the thing that actually diagnoses it. "It used an absolute word" is a reader telling you, in plain English, that the vocabulary and not the claim is carrying your labels. Run the keyword audit; it will almost certainly come out lopsided. Then rewrite items in the style of section 3 and re-probe. The labels are safe. The items are the problem, and no agreement figure would ever have said so.

(b) 17/20 = 85%. Inside the 70–90% band, which is where you want to be. Proceed — but only after going through all three disagreements individually. For each: either rewrite the item so the intended reading is unambiguous, keep the label and write down in notes the rule that justifies it, or cut it and replace it so the 20/20 balance survives. Report 85% as the pre-resolution figure, with its Wilson interval of [64.0%, 94.8%] so the reader can see how imprecise it is, and mention that three items were resolved by discussion.

(c) 12/20 = 60%. Below the 70% line. Stop, and do not run Phase 4. Two people cannot agree what these items mean, so any model score would be measuring the disagreement rather than the model. Sit down with the reader, go through the disputed items out loud, and find out whether the problem is the items, the misconception statements, or the one-sentence instruction you gave them. If a whole misconception is generating the confusion, drop that misconception and write four new items under a different one.

(d) 19/20 with sentences about claims. The healthiest of the four, and the one worth studying. 95% is above the 90% line, so the rule sends you to the evidence rather than waving you through — and the evidence is good: a reader who writes "had to decide whether rotation actually helps here" is reasoning about the claim, not reading the surface. Combined with a non-perfect probe, that is an acceptable pass. Rewrite the one disputed item to supply the missing context, or cut and replace it. Note that your own prior suspicion and an independent reader landed on the same item, which is evidence your judgement about difficulty is calibrated. Kappa here is $2(0.95) - 1 = 0.90$ on a balanced subset — report it in the write-up, and do not let it do any deciding.

Exercise 3.4 ★★arithmetic

You and a second reader both label 20 items. Compute Cohen's kappa, showing every step, and say in one sentence what it means.

reader said Mreader said C
you said M82
you said C19
Solution

Step 1 — observed agreement. The diagonal is where you agreed: $8 + 9 = 17$ out of $8+2+1+9 = 20$. So $p_o = 17/20 = 0.85$.

Step 2 — the marginals. You said M on $8 + 2 = 10$ items, so $a = 10/20 = 0.50$. The reader said M on $8 + 1 = 9$ items, so $b = 9/20 = 0.45$.

Step 3 — chance agreement. $p_e = ab + (1-a)(1-b) = (0.50)(0.45) + (0.50)(0.55) = 0.225 + 0.275 = 0.50$.

Step 4 — kappa. $\kappa = \dfrac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} = \dfrac{0.85 - 0.50}{0.50} = \dfrac{0.35}{0.50} = \mathbf{0.70}$.

Check. Your marginal is exactly $a = 0.5$, so the shortcut applies: $2p_o - 1 = 2(0.85) - 1 = 0.70$. Agreed.

Meaning. "Substantial" agreement on the Landis–Koch convention cited. Note that the raw 85% figure and the $\kappa = 0.70$ figure are the same fact stated twice; kappa is not more accurate, it is just harder to oversell.

And where it goes. This sentence belongs in your write-up, not at the gate. The gate reads the raw 85%, sees it inside the 70–90% band, and sends you to look at the three disagreements and the "what decided it" sentences. Kappa is computed on the same 20 items and inherits the same imprecision — the Wilson interval on 17/20 is [64.0%, 94.8%] — so promoting it to a decision rule would be dressing up a wide interval in a decimal point. Compute it, report it, do not decide on it.

Exercise 3.5 ★★thinking

A classmate reads section 2 and proposes a shortcut: "the mock model is a nine-word keyword rule, so its accuracy on your forty items tells you how well nine words separate them. High mock accuracy means keyword-separable, so just run --mock and read the number." (a) Say precisely why this does not work, using the flip rule from section 2. (b) They then propose training a classifier on the forty items instead. Why does that fail too? (c) What replaces both, and why is it worth the two minutes?

Solution

(a) The argument is sound right up to the point where the mock stops being a keyword rule. After the nine-word lookup it flips its own verdict whenever $(7L + 13E) \bmod 10 \lt 3$, where $L$ is the character count and $E$ the number of e's, so a variable fraction of every run is decided by the length and letter mix of the text rather than by any word in it. The measured consequence is in section 2: on a deliberately keyword-separable eight-item set the mock scored 0.750, and simply appending trailing spaces to every item — which cannot change how separable the wording is — moved that between 0.500 and 0.875, overlapping the 0.375-to-0.625 range of a deliberately tell-free set. observed A reading you can shift by adding whitespace is not a reading of your dataset.

(b) Not enough data. A TF-IDF logistic regression under four-fold cross-validation on the twelve demo items came out between 0.417 and 0.500 with individual folds at 0.333 — at or below chance. observed That is not evidence the items are clean; it is the classifier failing to learn anything from nine training examples. Forty items gives it thirty, which is not a different situation in any way that matters. And notice the direction of the error: a classifier that always fails would report "your dataset is fine" about every dataset, including the worst one you could write. A test that cannot fail is not a test.

(c) The ten-item probe of section 8: the real model, your real items, both prompts, twenty calls, two minutes. It sidesteps the whole problem by not trying to predict whether the model can separate your items and simply asking it. It is also the only version that is run early enough to be actionable — at ten items rather than forty — which is what makes it cheap. The keyword audit in section 9 is the surviving remnant of the automated idea: crude, five minutes by eye, and it catches the one family of tells that actually shows up.

Worth carrying forward from this exercise: two plausible diagnostics were built and both were discarded, and the honest write-up of that is more useful than a diagnostic that quietly returns noise. Reporting a tool that did not work is a normal part of research.

Exercise 3.6 ★by hand

Here are three rows a student typed straight into a text editor. Which will load, which will crash, and which is the dangerous one? Fix each broken row.

R11,M03,M,MFA means nobody can get into my account ever., R12,M03,C,It helps a lot, but SIM swapping is still a real risk.,worth a second read R13,M03,C,"I use an app rather than SMS, because SMS codes can be intercepted.",
Solution

R11 loads. Five fields, no commas inside any of them, empty notes. Fine as written.

R12 is broken. The comma after "a lot" splits response_text into two fields, so the row has six where the header promised five, and pandas raises ParserError: Error tokenizing data. C error: Expected 5 fields in line N, saw 6. Fix by quoting the text field:

R12,M03,C,"It helps a lot, but SIM swapping is still a real risk.",worth a second read

R13 loads. It has a comma, but the field is quoted, so the parser knows the comma is content rather than a separator. This is the correct pattern.

The dangerous one. R12, but not because it crashes — a crash is the good outcome, since it tells you immediately. The dangerous case is the same mistake made in the first data row: pandas may then infer the wider shape and silently shift every field one column left, so ground_truth ends up holding a sentence fragment and the run proceeds on nonsense. load_responses() catches that by refusing any label that is not exactly M or C, which is why that check is worth its four lines. Easiest prevention of all: type the whole thing in a spreadsheet and export as CSV, and never hand-quote anything.

Exercise 3.7 ★★★thinking

Suppose your dataset turns out easy enough that the baseline prompt scores 39 of 40. (a) What is the largest improvement the misconception-aware prompt could possibly show, in items and in percentage points? (b) The 95% Wilson interval for 39/40 is [87.1%, 99.6%]. Compare its width to the answer in (a). (c) What does this tell you about the value of the study, and what is the fix?

Solution

(a) The aware prompt cannot score more than 40 of 40, so the maximum possible improvement is 1 item, which is $1/40 = 2.5$ percentage points. That is the entire effect size available to you, before you have run anything.

(b) The interval on 39/40 alone is $99.6 - 87.1 = 12.5$ points wide. The largest effect the design could possibly show is 2.5 points, so the whole measurable effect is about one fifth of the uncertainty on a single condition. (The paired comparison in Phase 5 is more sensitive than comparing two intervals, but a one-item difference is still a single flipped item, and a single flipped item is not a finding.)

(c) The study is dead on arrival: the dataset has left no room for an effect to appear in, so both "the catalogue helps" and "the catalogue does not help" would produce roughly the same numbers, and you cannot tell them apart. This is Lab 1's right-hand end, in arithmetic rather than in pictures.

The fix is a dataset fix, not an analysis fix, and no amount of cleverness in Phase 5 recovers it. Rewrite items until the baseline lands somewhere in the middle of the range — the sweet spot on the Lab 1 curve is around a quarter of the way along, where the baseline is well above the 50% floor but well short of the ceiling. That is also, not coincidentally, where your second reader has to stop and think.

One honest caveat worth writing in your discussion: you do not get to tune the dataset after seeing the baseline's real score and then report the difference as if it were a clean prediction. Do the hardening now, on the basis of the ten-item probe and the second reader, and freeze the dataset before the run in Phase 4. The probe is deliberately designed to be usable this way: it runs on ten items you have not yet built a study around, it prints no metric you could be tempted to report, and it happens before the other thirty exist. Deciding what counts as good data after you have seen the answer is how honest people produce dishonest results.

Going deeper (optional)

More than two readers. Cohen's kappa handles exactly two. For three or more, or for readers who did not all label the same items, the usual measures are Fleiss' kappa and Krippendorff's alpha. cited You do not need either here, and reaching for them on 20 items with 2 readers would be decoration. Knowing the names is worth two minutes; implementing them is not.

Adjudication. In larger studies disagreements are not thrown out: a third reader settles them, and the paper reports the pre-adjudication agreement alongside the final labels. You do a miniature version of this whenever you rewrite an item after a disagreement. What makes it legitimate rather than fudging is deciding the rule before you look at which way the disagreement went, and reporting the pre-adjudication number too.

Where the ceiling effect gets its name. It is standard vocabulary in psychometrics for a test on which too many people score at or near the maximum, so the test cannot rank them. cited Your forty items are the test and the two prompts are the two candidates taking it — which is the sentence to be able to say at the check-in without looking at this page.
