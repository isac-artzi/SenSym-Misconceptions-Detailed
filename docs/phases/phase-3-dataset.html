Wrong in a Particular Way · Phase 3
The projectGate: the dataset either works or gets rebuilt

Forty responses, and the trap in them

Writing the only part of this project nobody else can write for you, in a way that can actually measure something.

Everything upstream of here is installation and typing. This is the phase where the study is either made or quietly destroyed, and the destruction is silent: the numbers come out looking excellent, both conditions score in the nineties, and the experiment has measured nothing but your own writing habits. This page is mostly about not doing that. Budget two weeks, and expect to throw some items away.

By the end of this phase you can

  • Explain what a ceiling effect is, and say out loud why it would end this study.
  • Write a misconception item that is realistic, plausible, and about exactly one idea.
  • Recognise a "hard" item — correct-sounding but wrong, or wrong-sounding but correct — and say why those are worth ten easy ones.
  • Justify 20 M and 20 C, and state the majority-class baseline that balance buys you.
  • Run a blind second labelling, compute percentage agreement, and compute Cohen's kappa from a 2×2 table.
  • Fill in responses.csv and misconceptions.csv without the comma trap eating a row.
  • Decide, with a stated rule rather than a feeling, whether the dataset is ready for Phase 4.

1 · The trap this page exists to prevent

Here is the failure, written out plainly so you can recognise it before it happens.

You sit down to write twenty responses containing a misconception. Because you know which ones are supposed to be wrong, you write them wrong. They fill up with always, never, impossible, 100% safe. Then you write twenty correct responses, and because you know those are supposed to be right, they come out careful and hedged: it depends, generally, although. Every M item shouts and every C item murmurs.

Now run the study. The baseline prompt scores 97%. The misconception-aware prompt scores 97%. You have not discovered that the catalogue does not help. You have discovered that a three-billion-parameter model can tell shouting from murmuring, which was never in doubt. The two conditions cannot differ, because there is no room above 97% for the better one to occupy. That is a ceiling effect, and it is the single most common way a small study like this produces a month of work and no finding.

The rule to internalise

If your model scores near 100% under both prompts, suspect your dataset before you celebrate. An experiment can only detect a difference in the space between the floor and the ceiling. Your floor is fixed at 50% by the design (section 4). Your ceiling is set entirely by how hard your items are, and that is the one thing you control this phase.

Lab · the ceiling-effect simulator

One slider: how obviously different your twenty M items are from your twenty C items. Push it right and both prompts climb toward 100%. Watch the shaded band — the space between the two conditions, which is the only thing your experiment can measure — get squeezed out of existence. Read the last line of the readout at each setting. It says what you would be entitled to conclude.

Two things in that picture are worth saying in words. First, the intervals are enormous: at n = 40 a 95% Wilson interval is roughly twenty-five percentage points wide in the middle of the range, so the honest question is never "did the aware prompt win" but "did it win by enough to see through that". Second, two intervals overlapping does not by itself mean there is no difference — both conditions run over the same forty items, so Phase 5's comparison is paired and more sensitive than eyeballing two intervals. None of that helps if the gap is zero. No test can find a difference your dataset made impossible.

2 · What a good item looks like

Five properties. Each one has a test you can apply in about five seconds.

PropertyThe test
RealisticCould a real tenth-grader have written this in a real class? Read it aloud. If it sounds like a textbook or like a cartoon, it fails.
PlausibleDoes the wrong one contain a grain of truth pushed too far? A misconception people actually hold is never absurd — that is why they hold it.
A student's voiceFirst person, contractions, a specific detail. "My password is 24 characters" beats "Passwords should be long".
One misconceptionIf an item contains two errors, and the model catches one, is that M? You will not be able to answer consistently across forty items, so do not create the problem.
Long enough to reason aboutRoughly one to three sentences. Under a sentence there is nothing to detect; over three you are testing reading comprehension instead.

The twelve demo items in python/data/sample/responses.csv are written to that standard, so they are a useful target even though no number computed from them is ever a result. Here are four of them beside a weak rewrite of the same idea — the version you will write by accident at eleven at night with nine items left to go.

TruthWeak — do not write thisGood — the demo set's versionWhat the weak one leaks
M "A really long password can never ever be cracked, it is 100% impossible." "My password is 24 characters so it's basically uncrackable. Length is the only thing that really matters for password strength." (S01) Three absolute words in one sentence. A keyword matcher wins without reading.
C "Password strength is a function of entropy over the character set, per NIST SP 800-63B." "Length helps a lot, but a long password made of common words can still be guessed by a dictionary attack. It needs to be long and unpredictable." (S03) Nobody in your grade talks like that. The register alone marks it C.
M "Changing your password always makes you completely safe, guaranteed." "Our school makes us reset our password every 60 days, which is the main reason school accounts are more secure than personal ones." (S06) The weak one is a slogan. C "MFA is good but not perfect." "Two-factor is the single best thing I've turned on, though I switched from SMS codes to an authenticator app because SMS can be intercepted." (S12) Six words is not enough to reason about. There is no claim there to be right or wrong about.
A diagnostic you can actually run — and it is free

The fake model in --mock mode is not a model at all. Open python/misconception/llm_client.py and read ask_llm_mock: it looks for nine words — always, never, impossible, guarantee, completely, definitely, can't be, cannot be, safe from — says M if it finds one, then deliberately flips about 30% of its own answers so the confusion matrix has all four boxes filled in.

So run --mock on your forty items. If the keyword rule is right on a fraction $p$ of them, the mock's reported accuracy is about

$$a_{\text{mock}} = 0.7p + 0.3(1-p) = 0.3 + 0.4p, \qquad\text{so}\qquad p \approx \frac{a_{\text{mock}} - 0.3}{0.4}.$$

A well-written dataset should leave the keyword rule near chance, $p \approx 0.5$, which shows up as a mock accuracy near 50%. A mock accuracy near 70% means $p \approx 1$: nine words are separating your two groups, and so will anything else. observed The flip is deterministic rather than random, so on any particular forty items the realised rate is only near 0.3 — treat this as a smoke alarm, not a measurement.

3 · The hard cases are the only valuable ones

An easy item is one where both prompts get it right. It contributes to both accuracies equally and to the difference between them not at all. Forty easy items produce two identical numbers and no study. The entire signal in this experiment lives in the items that one prompt gets and the other does not, and those are, necessarily, the hard ones. Aim for roughly a third of your set to be items you had to think about yourself.

There are two kinds, and you want both.

A wrong response that sounds right

"Our school makes us reset our password every 60 days, which is the main reason school accounts are more secure than personal ones." (S06, truth = M)

Calm, institutional, no absolutes, and completely mistaken. Scheduled rotation is the belief being tested: NIST SP 800-63B recommends against requiring periodic password changes, because forced rotation pushes people toward predictable increments and does nothing if the credential has already leaked. cited A model that detects this has done something a keyword matcher cannot. A model that misses it produces a false negative, and false negatives are what recall is about.

A correct response that sounds wrong

"I only change a password if there's a reason to — a breach notice or a login I don't recognize. Rotating on a schedule just makes people pick predictable patterns." (S07, truth = C)

Read quickly, this sounds like a student defending laziness, and a naive reader marks it M. It is right, and for the right reason. This item is the mirror of S06 and belongs in the set precisely because it punishes a model that has learned "mentions not changing passwords → M" instead of learning the actual claim. Items like this are where a false positive comes from, and false positives are what precision is about.

Both of those are hard. Neither is unlabelable, and the difference between those two words is the whole of section 6. Here is one that crosses the line:

Hard, versus impossible — do not confuse them

"I write all my passwords in a notebook I keep in a drawer at home, and they're all different."

Is that M or C? It depends entirely on the threat model. Against a remote attacker it is fine and arguably good practice; against someone with physical access to the house it is not. There is no defensible single label, so a second reader will disagree with you no matter what you choose, and the item measures the coin-flip rather than the model.

Hard means a thoughtful reader agrees with you after thinking. Impossible means a thoughtful reader disagrees with you after thinking. Keep the first, cut the second. If you love an item like this, the fix is to add the missing context to the item itself — make the student mention the threat they have in mind — not to argue for your label.

4 · Balance, and why exactly twenty and twenty

Twenty M and twenty C, four responses for each of ten misconceptions: two M and two C per misconception. That structure is doing two separate jobs, and both are worth being able to defend out loud.

Job one: it fixes the floor. The dumbest possible model ignores the text entirely and always guesses whichever label is more common. Its accuracy is the majority-class baseline:

$$\text{majority baseline} \;=\; \frac{\max(n_M,\, n_C)}{n_M + n_C}.$$

With 20 and 20 that is exactly $20/40 = 0.5$. Every number you report afterwards can be read against a floor of 50% that a reader does not have to be told twice. Let it drift to 28 M and 12 C and the floor moves to $28/40 = 70\%$: a model scoring 68% is now performing worse than a rock, and an unwary reader who sees "68% accuracy" thinks it did fine. load_responses() in run_experiment.py prints a warning when $|n_M - n_C| > 0.3n$ for exactly this reason, but a warning is not a substitute for getting it right.

Job two: it stops the topic from doing the work. This is the subtler one. Suppose all your password-length items are M and all your MFA items are C. A model can then score brilliantly by recognising the topic and never engaging with the claim, and you would have no way to tell that apart from real detection. Putting two M and two C on every misconception means the topic carries no information about the label, so the only thing left to distinguish them is the thing you actually care about.

The positive class, stated once and used everywhere

Throughout this project the positive class is M: "this response contains a misconception." Every metric is with respect to it.

$$\text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}.$$

Recall is the headline: of the misconceptions that really were there, what fraction did the model catch. A tutor that stays silent while a student holds a dangerous belief has done the expensive kind of damage. If a denominator is zero the metric is undefined, not zero — G.metrics returns null and prints "undefined", and you should write it that way too.

6 · The second reader, and how much you agreed

You are the worst possible judge of whether your own labels are obvious, because you wrote them and you remember what you meant. So hand a subset to somebody else — a friend, a parent, a sibling, the mentor — and have them label it blind.

How to run it properly

Take at least 10 of your 40 items, ideally with equal numbers of your M and your C. Strip the ground_truth column. Give them the misconception catalogue and one sentence of instruction: "mark M if this response contains one of these misconceptions, C if it does not." Do not sit next to them. Do not explain any item. Collect their labels, then compare.

If a human cannot reliably agree with you, the model has no chance, and the problem is the label rather than the model. That sentence is the reason this step exists, and it is worth a line in your write-up whichever way it comes out.

Percentage agreement

The simple version, and a perfectly respectable thing to report:

$$p_o \;=\; \frac{\text{items you both gave the same label}}{\text{items you both labelled}}.$$

Say you both label 10 items and agree on 9. Then $p_o = 0.9$, and you write "the second rater agreed with my label on 9 of 10 items (90%)". Nothing wrong with that. The weakness is that it flatters you: with two labels, two people guessing at random agree about half the time, so 90% is really only most of the way from 50 to 100, not from 0 to 100.

Cohen's kappa cited

Cohen's kappa is the standard fix. It subtracts off the agreement you would expect by chance and rescales what is left, so that 0 means "no better than chance" and 1 means "perfect". cited (Cohen, 1960, A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales, Educational and Psychological Measurement 20(1), 37–46.)

$$\kappa \;=\; \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}, \qquad p_e \;=\; ab + (1-a)(1-b),$$

where $a$ is the fraction of items you called M and $b$ is the fraction the second reader called M. The $p_e$ term is the probability two people would land on the same label by luck alone, given how often each of them says M.

A shortcut that is exactly true for a balanced subset proved

If your half of the subset is exactly balanced — you labelled as many M as C, so $a = \tfrac{1}{2}$ — then

$$p_e = \tfrac{1}{2}b + \tfrac{1}{2}(1-b) = \tfrac{1}{2} \qquad\text{whatever the second reader does,}$$

and substituting into the definition gives

$$\kappa = \frac{p_o - \tfrac12}{1 - \tfrac12} = 2p_o - 1.$$

So on a balanced subset, 90% agreement is $\kappa = 0.8$, 85% is $0.7$, 80% is $0.6$, and 70% is $0.4$. Two lines of algebra, and it means you can sanity-check any kappa you compute in your head. It also shows why kappa is the more honest number: 70% agreement sounds respectable and $\kappa = 0.4$ does not, and $\kappa = 0.4$ is telling the truth.

Worked, on ten items

You and your reader both label 10 items. You called 5 of them M; they called 4 M. You agree on 9 (both said M on 4 items, both said C on 5), and on the tenth you said M and they said C.

$p_o = 9/10 = 0.9$.   $a = 5/10 = 0.5$,   $b = 4/10 = 0.4$.

$p_e = (0.5)(0.4) + (0.5)(0.6) = 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.50$.

$\kappa = (0.9 - 0.5)/(1 - 0.5) = 0.4/0.5 = \mathbf{0.80}$ — which is what the shortcut above predicts, since $2(0.9) - 1 = 0.8$.

The conventional reading of kappa is Landis and Koch's: 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1.00 almost perfect. cited (Landis & Koch, 1977, Biometrics 33(1), 159–174.) Those bands are convention, not mathematics — nothing computes them, people agreed on them — so quote them as a convention and not as a law. For this project, aim for $\kappa \geq 0.6$, which on a balanced subset means agreeing on at least 8 of 10.

If kappa is one step too far

Report raw percentage agreement and say so plainly: "a second rater labelled 10 of the 40 responses without seeing my labels; we agreed on 9 (90%)." That is honest, checkable, and more than most work at this level does. What is not acceptable is skipping the second reader and asserting that the labels are obvious. The number is small; the absence of the number is not.

The result you must not misread

Perfect agreement is not automatically good news. If your reader agrees on 10 of 10 and says it was easy, you have evidence for the section 1 problem, not against it. So ask them the second question: "which ones did you have to think about?" If the answer is "none of them", go back and make some items harder. Write their answer down; it is data.

7 · The two files, and the comma that eats them

Both files already exist in python/data/ with placeholder rows starting <. The loaders drop any row whose text field still begins with that character, so a half-finished file behaves sensibly instead of crashing.

misconceptions.csv — exactly 10 rows, four columns:

misconception_id,statement,why_wrong,real_world_consequence

responses.csv — exactly 40 rows, five columns:

response_id,misconception_id,ground_truth,response_text,notes
ColumnWhat goes in it
response_idR01 to R40. Must be unique; the loader stops on duplicates.
misconception_idM01–M10, matching a row in misconceptions.csv.
ground_truthCapital M or capital C, nothing else, no trailing space. This column is your entire experiment.
response_textThe student's answer, one to three sentences. The only field the model ever sees.
notesFor you. Why you labelled it that way, what made it hard, who else read it. The pipeline never reads this column, so write freely — and your Phase 5 self will thank you.
Save as CSV, not xlsx, and mind the commas

Writing these in Excel or Google Sheets is the right move: type normally, and the quoting is handled for you on export. But you must File → Save As → CSV. An .xlsx sitting in python/data/ is invisible to the loader, which opens responses.csv by name and will tell you the file is not there.

If you type a row by hand in a text editor, any field containing a comma has to be wrapped in double quotes. Miss one and you get a row with too many fields:

R08,M02,C,I only change a password if there is a reason to, like a breach notice.,

which pandas counts as six fields where the header promised five:

pandas.errors.ParserError: Error tokenizing data. C error: Expected 5 fields in line 9, saw 6

The fix is one pair of quotes:

R08,M02,C,"I only change a password if there is a reason to, like a breach notice.",

The nastier version of this bug does not crash. If the stray comma is in the first data row, pandas can quietly shift every field one column left, so ground_truth ends up holding a fragment of somebody's sentence. That is why load_responses() checks every label against {M, C} and stops with the offending response_id rather than running eighty model calls on garbage.

8 · The gate

Rebuilding here is a normal outcome, not a failure, and it is far cheaper than rebuilding after two runs and a written-up analysis. It usually means rewriting a handful of items, not starting over.

Run these before you say you are done 
$ cd python
$ python3 misconception/check_setup.py
$ python3 misconception/run_experiment.py --mock
$ python3 misconception/analyze.py
$ pytest ../ -q

Note the missing --sample: this run uses your forty items with the fake model. You are not looking at whether the numbers are good — they are fake and cannot be good. You are looking for two things: that all forty rows load with the counts (20 misconception, 20 correct), and that the mock accuracy is near 50% rather than near 70%, per the diagnostic in section 2.

Before the check-in — the gate criteria
  • misconceptions.csv has 10 real rows and no <placeholder> text left.
  • responses.csv has 40 real rows, and the run prints (20 misconception, 20 correct).
  • Every misconception has exactly 2 M and 2 C written about it.
  • Every item is one to three sentences, in a student's voice, about one misconception.
  • I read all forty aloud and none of them sounded like a robot or a slogan.
  • At least a third of the items are ones I had to think about myself.
  • --mock on my own data scores near 50%, not near 70%.
  • A second reader labelled at least 10 items blind; I have the number, and $\kappa \geq 0.6$ or agreement $\geq 8/10$.
  • I asked the second reader which items were hard, and wrote down what they said.
  • Items where we disagreed have been rewritten, relabelled with a written reason, or cut.

9 · Exercises

Exercise 3.1 ★★writing

Take misconception M02, "changing passwords often makes accounts safer." Write one hard M item and one hard C item for it. Hard means: a thoughtful reader will get it right, but only after stopping. Then write one sentence for each saying what makes it hard.

Solution

Hard M: "I keep the same base password but add the month on the end and change it every 30 days, so even if one gets leaked it's already out of date by the time anyone tries it."

What makes it hard: there are no absolutes anywhere in it, the student sounds thoughtful and organised, and the sentence contains a real security concept (a leaked credential going stale). The error is buried in the mechanism — a predictable increment is trivially guessable once one version leaks, so the rotation buys almost nothing. A keyword matcher sees "leaked", "changed", "30 days" and has no basis for a verdict.

Hard C: "I haven't changed my email password in two years and I'm not planning to. It's long, random, only used on that one site, and nothing has shown up in a breach check."

What makes it hard: it sounds like textbook negligence, and it opens with the exact behaviour a naive reader has been taught to flag. It is correct: with a unique, high-entropy credential and no evidence of compromise, scheduled rotation adds no security, which is the position NIST SP 800-63B takes. cited

Notice that this pair is genuinely mirrored: the M item changes its password and is wrong, the C item refuses to and is right. That mirroring is what stops the model from scoring by keyword, and it is the thing to aim for on all ten misconceptions.

Exercise 3.2 ★arithmetic

You run out of time and submit 28 M items and 12 C items. (a) What is the majority-class baseline? (b) A model that outputs M for every single item without reading it — what are its accuracy, recall and precision, with M as the positive class? (c) Why is this a problem for the write-up even if your model scores 75%?

Solution

(a) $\max(28,12)/40 = 28/40 = 0.70$, so the floor is 70%.

(b) Always-M gives $TP = 28$, $FN = 0$, $FP = 12$, $TN = 0$. Accuracy $= (28+0)/40 = 70\%$. Recall $= 28/(28+0) = 100\%$ — it catches every misconception, by refusing to ever say C. Precision $= 28/(28+12) = 70\%$. A model with perfect recall that has learned nothing at all.

(c) Because 75% is now only five points above a rock, and you have to say so. Worse, the headline metric of this study is recall, and the degenerate model has perfect recall — so on an unbalanced set a high recall no longer distinguishes a good model from a broken one. Balance is what keeps recall meaningful. With 20 and 20, always-M gives accuracy 50%, recall 100%, precision 50%, and the 50% precision immediately gives it away.

Exercise 3.3 ★★thinking

Your second reader labels 10 items blind. Decide, for each case, what you do next and why. (a) They agree on 10 of 10 and say it took five minutes. (b) They agree on 8 of 10. (c) They agree on 6 of 10. (d) They agree on 9 of 10, but the one disagreement is an item you already suspected was ambiguous.

Solution

(a) 10/10, easy. The labels are safe and the items may not be. Perfect effortless agreement is exactly what a ceiling-effect dataset produces. Go back and ask them which items required thought; if the answer is "none", rewrite a handful of items to be harder, in the style of section 3, and re-run the diagnostic in section 2. Do not treat 10/10 as a green light on its own.

(b) 8/10. On a balanced subset $\kappa = 2(0.8) - 1 = 0.6$, which just reaches the target. Proceed, but look at both disagreements individually. For each: either rewrite the item so the intended reading is unambiguous, keep the label and write down in notes the rule that justifies it, or cut it. Report the agreement figure in your methods either way.

(c) 6/10. $\kappa = 2(0.6) - 1 = 0.2$ — barely above chance. Stop. Do not run the experiment. Two people cannot agree what these items mean, so any model score is measuring the disagreement rather than the model. Sit down with the reader, go through all ten out loud, find out whether the problem is the items, the misconception statements, or the one-sentence instruction you gave them, and fix that before writing anything else.

(d) 9/10 on a known-ambiguous item. The healthiest of the four. Your own suspicion and an independent reader landed on the same item, which is evidence that your judgement about difficulty is calibrated. Rewrite that one item to supply the missing context, or cut it and write a replacement so the 20/20 balance survives. Report $\kappa = 0.8$ and mention the rewrite.

Exercise 3.4 ★★arithmetic

You and a second reader both label 20 items. Compute Cohen's kappa, showing every step, and say in one sentence what it means.

reader said Mreader said C
you said M82
you said C19
Solution

Step 1 — observed agreement. The diagonal is where you agreed: $8 + 9 = 17$ out of $8+2+1+9 = 20$. So $p_o = 17/20 = 0.85$.

Step 2 — the marginals. You said M on $8 + 2 = 10$ items, so $a = 10/20 = 0.50$. The reader said M on $8 + 1 = 9$ items, so $b = 9/20 = 0.45$.

Step 3 — chance agreement. $p_e = ab + (1-a)(1-b) = (0.50)(0.45) + (0.50)(0.55) = 0.225 + 0.275 = 0.50$.

Step 4 — kappa. $\kappa = \dfrac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} = \dfrac{0.85 - 0.50}{0.50} = \dfrac{0.35}{0.50} = \mathbf{0.70}$.

Check. Your marginal is exactly $a = 0.5$, so the shortcut applies: $2p_o - 1 = 2(0.85) - 1 = 0.70$. Agreed.

Meaning. "Substantial" agreement on the Landis–Koch convention cited — comfortably past the 0.6 target, so the dataset passes this criterion. Note that the raw 85% figure and the $\kappa = 0.70$ figure are the same fact stated twice; kappa is not more accurate, it is just harder to oversell.

Exercise 3.5 ★★arithmetic

You run run_experiment.py --mock on your own forty items and analyze.py reports an accuracy of 67.5% (27 of 40) for both conditions. (a) Using the relation from section 2, roughly how many of your items does the nine-word keyword rule get right? (b) What should you do? (c) Why are both conditions identical in mock mode, and is that a bug?

Solution

(a) $p \approx (a_{\text{mock}} - 0.3)/0.4 = (0.675 - 0.3)/0.4 = 0.375/0.4 = 0.9375$, which on forty items is about 37 or 38 of them. Nine words — always, never, impossible, guarantee, completely, definitely, can't be, cannot be, safe from — are separating your two groups almost perfectly.

(b) Rebuild. Search your response_text column for those nine strings, and for every hit either delete the absolute word or move it into a C item where it is used correctly ("no password is completely safe, so I also use MFA"). A real model will find that vocabulary at least as easily as the keyword rule did, both prompts will score in the nineties, and you will be at the right-hand end of the Lab 1 picture with nothing to report. Re-run the mock and aim for something near 50%.

(c) Not a bug. The comment in ask_llm_mock says so explicitly: the fake model ignores the prompt it was given and looks only at the response text, so there is nothing for the two prompts to influence. Identical conditions in mock mode are the expected result; conditions that diverge under --mock would mean something was genuinely wrong. The point of the mock run is the plumbing and this diagnostic, never the numbers.

Exercise 3.6 ★by hand

Here are three rows a student typed straight into a text editor. Which will load, which will crash, and which is the dangerous one? Fix each broken row.

R11,M03,M,MFA means nobody can get into my account ever., R12,M03,C,It helps a lot, but SIM swapping is still a real risk.,worth a second read R13,M03,C,"I use an app rather than SMS, because SMS codes can be intercepted.",
Solution

R11 loads. Five fields, no commas inside any of them, empty notes. Fine as written.

R12 is broken. The comma after "a lot" splits response_text into two fields, so the row has six where the header promised five, and pandas raises ParserError: Error tokenizing data. C error: Expected 5 fields in line N, saw 6. Fix by quoting the text field:

R13 loads. It has a comma, but the field is quoted, so the parser knows the comma is content rather than a separator. This is the correct pattern.

The dangerous one. R12, but not because it crashes — a crash is the good outcome, since it tells you immediately. The dangerous case is the same mistake made in the first data row: pandas may then infer the wider shape and silently shift every field one column left, so ground_truth ends up holding a sentence fragment and the run proceeds on nonsense. load_responses() catches that by refusing any label that is not exactly M or C, which is why that check is worth its four lines. Easiest prevention of all: type the whole thing in a spreadsheet and export as CSV, and never hand-quote anything.

Exercise 3.7 ★★★thinking

Suppose your dataset turns out easy enough that the baseline prompt scores 39 of 40. (a) What is the largest improvement the misconception-aware prompt could possibly show, in items and in percentage points? (b) The 95% Wilson interval for 39/40 is [87.1%, 99.6%]. Compare its width to the answer in (a). (c) What does this tell you about the value of the study, and what is the fix?

Solution

(a) The aware prompt cannot score more than 40 of 40, so the maximum possible improvement is 1 item, which is $1/40 = 2.5$ percentage points. That is the entire effect size available to you, before you have run anything.

(b) The interval on 39/40 alone is $99.6 - 87.1 = 12.5$ points wide. The largest effect the design could possibly show is 2.5 points, so the whole measurable effect is about one fifth of the uncertainty on a single condition. (The paired comparison in Phase 5 is more sensitive than comparing two intervals, but a one-item difference is still a single flipped item, and a single flipped item is not a finding.)

(c) The study is dead on arrival: the dataset has left no room for an effect to appear in, so both "the catalogue helps" and "the catalogue does not help" would produce roughly the same numbers, and you cannot tell them apart. This is Lab 1's right-hand end, in arithmetic rather than in pictures.

The fix is a dataset fix, not an analysis fix, and no amount of cleverness in Phase 5 recovers it. Rewrite items until the baseline lands somewhere in the middle of the range — the sweet spot on the Lab 1 curve is around a quarter of the way along, where the baseline is well above the 50% floor but well short of the ceiling. That is also, not coincidentally, where your second reader has to stop and think.

One honest caveat worth writing in your discussion: you do not get to tune the dataset after seeing the baseline's real score and then report the difference as if it were a clean prediction. Do the hardening now, on the basis of the mock diagnostic and the second reader, and freeze the dataset before the run in Phase 4. Deciding what counts as good data after you have seen the answer is how honest people produce dishonest results.

  • One sentence answering: "if both prompts score 95%, what will you say about your dataset?"
Going deeper (optional)

More than two readers. Cohen's kappa handles exactly two. For three or more, or for readers who did not all label the same items, the usual measures are Fleiss' kappa and Krippendorff's alpha. cited You do not need either here, and reaching for them on 10 items with 2 readers would be decoration. Knowing the names is worth two minutes; implementing them is not.

Adjudication. In larger studies disagreements are not thrown out: a third reader settles them, and the paper reports the pre-adjudication agreement alongside the final labels. You do a miniature version of this whenever you rewrite an item after a disagreement. What makes it legitimate rather than fudging is deciding the rule before you look at which way the disagreement went, and reporting the pre-adjudication number too.

Where the ceiling effect gets its name. It is standard vocabulary in psychometrics for a test on which too many people score at or near the maximum, so the test cannot rank them. cited Your forty items are the test and the two prompts are the two candidates taking it — which is the sentence to be able to say at the check-in without looking at this page.
